My first two years in New York, I lived with three roommates on a block that was 15 mins away from any train station in a tiny 4B2B apartment with no kitchen countertop or space for a sofa, but a good number of mice. The national fair market rate for a 4BR apartment is [$2,492](https://ipropertymanagement.com/research/average-rent-by-year). Our rent was $7,200. Of course, I’m privileged compared to the median earner in NYC. Over the past 20 years, the rent-to-income (RTI) ratio for median earners has gone from 25% to [55%](https://www.realtor.com/research/nyc-q2-2025-rent/), almost double the recommended 30%.

The first problem is that the optimal strategy for landlords in an unregulated space is to enforce scarcity and build for luxury. In a city like NYC, there will always be demand for nicer and nicer apartments, especially when your apartment’s relative standing becomes part of your social status. Instead of optimizing for multi-tenant space, landlords optimize for flashy apartment features and high square footage. Rent stabilization is needed to incentivize building owners to build for more tenants and landlords to keep apartments functional and practical.

The other problem is how expensive it is to build in NYC. Regulation, insurance, and process all lead to exorbitant construction costs. In NYC, it takes an average of [2.5 years](https://cbcny.org/advocacy/why-it-costs-so-much-build-new-york-city-0?utm_source=chatgpt.com) to secure zoning changes, while it takes [6-9 months](https://www.miami.gov/Planning-Zoning-Land-Use/Rezones/Request-a-Rezone?utm_source=chatgpt.com) in cities like Miami. Mamdani’s focus is not only to manage rent prices but also to reduce housing construction hurdles. He’s fast tracking affordable housing projects and reducing the regulation around new buildings such as parking lot requirements. Not only will this help grow the supply of affordable housing, but it will also help landlords maintain their profit margins.

My biggest question is how Mamdani will incentivize apartment owners to invest in ongoing maintenance of apartments (See [New Yorker piece](https://www.newyorker.com/news/the-lede/would-zohran-mamdanis-rent-freeze-keep-rent-stabilized-apartments-empty) on rundown NYC apartments). Maintenance is expensive and can be hard to prioritize without the promise of higher future rent, especially given tenant-favoring policies that anchor tenants down in their apartments. Of course, there’s solutions to this - incentivizing landlords through tax cuts, enforcing basic maintenance of properties, and/or extending capital for renovations. What will be a good push for tenants will also be a good push for landlords, though - maintenance should be done gradually and efficiently without relying on exorbitant future rent prices.

Mamdani has proposed to build 200,000 new affordable housing units in NYC in the next ten years, offering housing for at least 2% of the city’s population. This will significantly address the housing crisis, while still being under 10% of NYC’s annual city budget at about $10B per year. The plan is relatively drastic but necessary to maintain the socioeconomic diversity that Jane Jacobs has taught us is critical to a thriving city.

I love living in East Village, a bike ride away from my VC-funded startup office yet surrounded by vibrant restaurants, local bodegas, and cozy cafes. It’s also home to four public housing developments. I’m optimistic that Mamdani’s push for affordable housing will help nurture my neighborhood and keep its culture and spirit alive.
