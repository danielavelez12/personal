I just finished reading Tracy Kidder’s biography about Paul Farmer, Mountains Beyond Mountains, after a co-worker recommended it to me as one of the sources of his healthcare inspiration. Paul Farmer helped start clinics across Haiti, Peru, and even in the prisons of Russia. Paul reduced the cost of medications and treatments, like for multi-drug resistant tuberculosis (MDR) from almost $20,000 to $125.

**How Paul Farmer approached his mission**

As much as Paul Farmer made a huge global impact, he didn’t have a specific grand vision from the start. He embarked first on improving the quality of life in Cange, Haiti, with one clinic called Zanmi Lasante, and slowly (over 10, 20 years) starting taking on larger chunks of work as they surfaced. He did hard unscalable work whenever necessary, and never stopped doing it, such as walking 8 hours to bring treatment to a patient or finding a watch in Miami for a patient who asked him for one. Paul set out to not only provide basic medical treatment to every patient, but ensure that every patient recovered fully. He funded the organization through grants and donor funds, and made sure no patient was turned away for not being able to pay.

Paul ended up growing PIH not because it was his master plan, but because the people were asking him for it. A group from Peru reached out to him, begging for Paul to establish a clinic there to help tackle the tuberculosis crisis, and later on the Russian government called him in as well. Paul would only expand the organization’s reach given he was able to dedicate the same amount of care as needed to each of his patients. As opposed to the methodical rational altruism perspective, Paul’s way of operating was principle-based and context-dependent.

Eventually, Paul trained thousands of healthcare workers globally, influenced policy at WHO, World Bank, and national governments, and created new models for medical education. His impact on the world came slowly and then all at once.

**The PIH team and its antics**

Paul was able to assemble a team of people that were both sharp and dedicated, by keeping his team small (just 4 other full-time people!) for most of the organization’s lifetime. Ophelia* he met while in Haiti, and after a couple years they decided to start working together on building up the organization and raising funding. Paul recruited another team member, Jim, who he met through med school at Harvard, after a set of long, windy conversations. Another member was their investor, Tom White, who had always been looking for a cause exactly like theirs to fund. Tom donated most of his personal fortune (about $50 million) to PIH. The team had no office for the first year or so, and would just hang out at each other’s apartments, staying up having philosophical conversations or arguing about political semantics.

The team was always all-in and aligned, not just because of their shared internal values, but also because of the established culture on the team. They had their own shared language, such as:

- AMC (areas of moral clarity) - situations where it’s actually very clear what ought to be done
- H for G (a hermeneutic of generosity) - a bit of grace for someone who you know has good intent
- TBMI (transnational bureaucrats managing inequality), people with clever arguments, or “well-formed stool”
- A “ninety-nine one hundred” was quitting on a nearly completed job

This made the team feel like a club. The author says, “Farmer had a way of creating a club that consisted just of him and you.”

*One of my favorite fun facts of Paul’s story is that Ophelia is the daughter of Roald Dahl, who sent her to Haiti to become a better person. Paul ended up having a fling with her and met some of his literature idols (e.g. Nathan Hawthorne) through her dad. I shipped them but it didn’t work out between them romantically.
