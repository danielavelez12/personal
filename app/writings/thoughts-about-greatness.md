The past few months, I’ve been immersed in biographies - Bill Gates, Steve Jobs, Obama, Phil Knight. I visited home and my parents and I revisited Edison and Ford’s estate in Fort Myers. Reaching the two year mark of my full-time career has made me increasingly anxious about my progress - am I tracking towards my life goals? Have I missed the train on potentially doing something big?

Coincidentally, I’ve spent the past few weeks without a phone (since it was tragically stolen), and it’s given me a lot of time to daydream. Startup ideas, or curiosities to pursue, or what I want my future company to look like. So combined with the angst is an excitement to do things.

In the spirit of anxious yet childlike wonder, here’s a few reflections after reading about the greats.

**Destiny vs. serendipity**

*“The truth is, I’ve never been a big believer in destiny… I suspect that God’s plan, whatever it is, works on a a scale too large to admit our mortal tribulations; that in a single lifetime, accidents and happenstance determine more than we care to admit; and that the best we can do is to try to align ourselves with what we feel is right and construct some meaning out of our confusion, and with grace and nerve play at each moment the hand that we’re dealt.”* - Obama

Obama didn’t have plans to run for presidency until he started getting a lot of press as a senator, following his public speeches about those affected by Hurricane Katrina. Even when he started seriously considering running, he wasn’t certain he had what it took. Bill Gates also points out the serendipitous factors that helped his success - his middle school was one of the first schools to give students access to a computer, where he learned to code in BASIC.

It’s strangely comforting to know that some things are outside of our control. Instead of searching for signs that I’m on the right path, I know that there might not *be* any deterministic signs. While I just keep doing what I’m drawn to, and putting myself in the right environments, I can wait for the circumstances to arise to play my cards.

**Persevering against the odds**

While finding himself by traveling the world, Phil Knight asked to talk with a legendary military leader - General Vo Nguyen Giáp of Vietnam. He asked Giáp a simple question: “How did you do it?” and the general responded: “We just kept going.” Knight started Nike (or “Blue Ribbon”) in 1964, and it wasn’t until the late 1970s that Nike began gaining significant market share. Henry Ford worked on developing cars for about 12-15 years before creating the Model T, the breakthrough vehicle that changed the automotive industry.

I constantly have to remind myself that success is a marathon, not a race. My bad habit is scrolling through fundraising announcements on LinkedIn or launch posts on Twitter and feeling behind. The truth is, making an impact on the world can take years and years of trial and error and consistent hard work, so I shouldn’t expect to reach “success” in the next few years. In a world with continuously growing opportunity and burgeoning markets, there’s time, and I’ll eventually find my life’s work to dedicate 10+ perseverant years to. (And with an abundance mindset, it’s way easier to feel happy for friends doing big things.)

**Feeling “special”**

Steve Jobs was adopted, and when he was young, his adoptive parents looked him in the eyes and reminded him that they specifically picked him out. Jobs was a problem child throughout school. When the teachers approached his parents, they responded as if it was only natural for a child like Steve to be a challenge.

As an only child, I have a “special snowflake” complex, but over the years growing up into an adult, I’ve tried to *not* act like I’m special. Why should I be allowed to break rules, and not others? Instead of nerding out about things or passionately discussing my work, I’ve tried to revert to normal topics that make others feel more at home. Despite my efforts, I’ve been rejected twice this year after series of dates due to talking or asking too much about work-related things.

More recently, my hot take has become: it’s okay to act like you’re special. Take your laptop out at the bar and read your book in the subway - it’s not performative, because you’re truly not like the others, and you do things genuinely. Ask too many questions and speak up more than you should - trust that you have wisdom to contribute. And maybe I’ll continue with my intensity and work-related curiosities on dates.

Of course, it’s important to remain respectful and humble. But I now believe if you plan on doing great things you have to think you’re a bit better than the others. The way to do this with grace is to believe you’re in service of something bigger than yourself.

**Ending thoughts**

Despite attempting to embody these learnings and grow more confident, I’m also becoming more aware over time of what my weaknesses and knowledge gaps are. This is a result of 1) enduring a lot of failures and 2) getting to work with people who are brilliant and experienced in ways I’m not. To be an adult is to live in the contradiction. High confidence, low ego. Believe you’re somehow the chosen one who will seize the day and persevere endlessly, but also know your limits and seek help from others. And while always striving for more, be grateful to have the basic necessities (health, home, financial stability) and great people (coworkers, friends, family) at your side.
