This post is a brain dump of takes I’ve formed over the years - mostly an exercise for myself to form stronger opinions, though still loosely held.

**1. First impressions matter.**

In the first two minutes of an interaction, people make up their mind about you. How did you come off to them, and how did you make them feel? This will shape how they think about you moving forward (even though this can be rewritten over time). At my current job, the team met me first back when we were all working out of the same investor’s office, and their first impression of me was that I worked hard, from the times they would see me coding away late at the office. I started my interviews with them on the right foot.

Of course, don’t optimize for first impressions, but be the type of person who can give a good first impression (and put in the last-mile work and thoughtfulness to make it happen). Even more, you’ll make people feel respected when you show your best side to them.

**2. Physical health is the most important driver for success.**

Teddy Roosevelt grew up as a sickly child with asthma, but overcame it through gymnastics and weight-lifting. He then became a lifelong advocate of exercise and the “strenuous life.” Musk, Cook, Bezos, and Gates all work out consistently and explicitly try to get at least 7 hours of sleep (well, 6 for Musk).

With low health and sleep, your ability to do hard things is capped. And in order to be great you have to do hard things, over and over again. I believe more a unit more of health is worth more than a unit more of agency, talent, drive, etc. in the long-run.

**3. After that, it’s all mindset.**

When I was working on my own startup, I realized things went significantly better whenever I fully believed: “We are building a great company.” when my mindset was right, it was easier to talk to potential customers, fundraise, and put in the day-to-day long hours. Believe first, then you will act accordingly, and outcomes will follow.

**4. Benevolent dictatorship is optimal**

Lee Kuan Yew, the first prime minister of Singapore, is often described as a benevolent dictator:

1. Though he wasn’t given official authoritarian power, he enforced it by maintaining near-total control through one-party dominance.
2. He was relatively benevolent, supporting long-term social and economic planning, meritocracy and multiracialism, and policies such as an English lingua franca combined with bilingualism in schools to preserve ethnic communities.

He transformed Singapore from a struggling nation into one of the world’s most prosperous and efficient economies.

Instead of checks and balances and systems of democracy that fail to serve everyone and add inefficiency, I believe rulers should be chosen extremely well and then given full reign (within the bounds of international law.)

**5. Good systems are built off of smaller systems**

Regardless of having one ruler at the top, good systems have to have subsystems (e.g. “authoritarian federalism”). Each subsystem should be defined its unique environment, constraints, and goals. At Tandem, we use 20+ separate services in our codebase in order to keep the overall system maintainable and easy to iterate on. Apart from governments and software, think about your personal system. It is much easier to create one habit and later connect another one, than build both from scratch.

**6. Things are only maintained if they’re lived in**

I’ve lived in apartments that fall apart because the residents are never home. I’ve seen codebase files that got a lot of love at the start, but were never touched again because engineers weren’t working in those files. Any efforts to coordinate cleanup and maintenance for a space will be futile if the people aren’t invested in it. To ensure something stays alive, make it part of someone’s world.

**7. Small talk helps set up a conversation**

Before heading into a conversation, it’s important to exchange trust first. You’re signaling to them that they’re safe in this conversation with you, while they’re signaling that back. It’s not necessarily the words said, but the way they’re said, that can set the other person at ease.

**8. Intention setting is worth it**

Conversations and relationships go wrong because of misaligned expectations. There’s many reasons people are afraid of making their intentions clear - they’re worried about coming off as cringe, desperate, or transactional. But if you come in with a mindset of abundance and respect, you’ll signal just the right thing and give the other person the clarity they’re seeking.

**9. Roles and context matter**

Similarly, people want clarity in how to interact with you and how to understand your actions towards them. If you’re friends with your roommates, it’s easier to switch on “roommate” mode before asking them to clear the sink, than to thread it into a friendly conversation. At work, oversharing can throw people off, because you’re acting out of line for just a co-worker. Better to focus on being a great co-worker during work hours, and vent to your co-worker friend after hours.

**10. If you hate someone, get to know them better**

It’s one thing to hate things that a person does (I think this is normal), and another to hate the person entirely. 10/10 times I’ve felt hatred towards someone it’s because I don’t know them well enough. And even if you don’t want this person in your life, it’s an opportunity to help you learn about yourself and fill the blindspots in your perspective and empathy.

**11. Question your own intentions**

I didn’t realize how many things I was doing for peer or societal validation, until I started questioning my intentions and asking myself, “how will I feel after I do this? Will this bring me fulfillment?” there’s lots of other underlying reasons we do things, and we’ll easily lie to ourselves, like telling yourself you’re scrolling on twitter to catch up on information when you’re actually just avoiding your anxiety. For every change of course* you take, check whether it’s right for you.

*Change of course meaning, if you’re locked in on one path to complete a project, then don’t question, just go.

**12. Work should feel hard**

I’ve been playing piano since I was 7 years old and I love it. But, it has always felt hard. There’s definitely times when I feel like I’m engaging in “play,” like when I get to add my own interpretation to a piece or jam with friends, but the times when I’m building up core muscle memory to play a new piece, it can feel tedious and frustrating. Real work changes you because it’s hard. And, the outcome is made significant and unique because others didn’t choose to put in that work.

**13. Remember people’s names**

Whenever I remember someone’s name and repeat it in conversation or when I see them again, their face lights up and the conversation flows. People love hearing their name. Even if you’re not planning on getting to know this person, you have to train your brain to write every name to memory.

**14. Be okay with your contradictions**

I’ve seen so many friends (and myself) being scared of becoming hypocrites or contradicting themselves. They won’t try something new because they don’t have the time to get good at it, or they’ll refrain from breaking their diet just out of principle. You can be a “high-achieving” person and be bad at some things, and you can live a “healthy” lifestyle and eat bar food once in a while. In fact, it’s the pressure to broadly label ourselves that’s the problem. Life is full of contradictions.

**15. Hold yourself to a higher standard than others**

Set expectations low for others and allow them to delight you. If they don’t meet your expectations, there’s often little you can do. Set expectations reasonably high for yourself, and if you don’t reach them, there’s a lot you can do.

**16. Don’t listen to the golden rule - “treat others how you would like to be treated”**

The way people want to be treated is usually different from the treatment they actually deserve, it’s either less or more. And the way they actually treat you back is also different. So don’t expect others to treat you like they want to be treated, and always treat others how they deserve to be treated.

**17. Stress stems from a fear of the unknown**

Our stress is an instinctual fear of something dangerous looming, like in those horror movies where the creature is never shown, just implied. My anxious procrastination always dissipates when I actually dive into a project and clarify what I have to do. Things will always start off uncertain, so creating clarity for yourself and how to move forward on something is the best way to tackle stress.

**18. Love means being present**

It’s easy to show up for people - send a text, attend the birthday party, or buy some roses. But it only matters if you’re present in these actions. Did you place yourself in their shoes while writing the text? Did you make them feel loved during the birthday party? Did you get them their favorite flowers? No matter the love language, what matters is presence.

**19. Love is a hack**

If you want to do anything right, just do it with love. It’s a type of purpose-setting that enhances your focus, skill, and attention. Cooking a meal? Put your heart and soul into it and you won’t overcook or miss any steps. Of course, this sets you up for heartbreak (if your meal wasn’t appreciated), but it’s a transitory feeling. Now you’re on your way to becoming a master chef.

**20. Language is the key to gaining status**

Language, for better or for worse, signals social status more than anything. It reveals how you were raised, what your line of work is, and how you think. I’ve seen how people gravitate towards certain communicators in the startup world - they’re clear and direct and reference companies or products whenever possible. Just think about how gamers talk on a discord channel or on twitch. Language is the easiest way to indicate that you’re part of a group.

**21. Observing someone’s self talk is seeing into their character**

If someone is always judging themselves, they’ll likely apply that to you as well. On the other hand, if someone is accepting of their flaws, proud of their wins, etc., they’ll likely be a great person to have as a friend. This comes through in their self-talk - how do they talk about themselves? How do they react when you make an observation about them? These are all nuggets of insight you can use to form a picture of someone.

**22. There’s no such thing as accidents**

I’m a super clumsy and careless person sometimes, and I recognize it’s part of my character that I’m working to improve. I’ve fallen off my bike multiple times, messed up people’s pronouns, and hurt my close friends. And I recognize them not as accidents, but signs of a habit or muscle I need to build.

**23. However, forgive easily**

People grow and mature. They also do things for reasons you can’t possibly understand, because you’re different people with separate world views, assumptions, and habits / muscles. If you care about their growth, share with them what the impact of their actions was. If not, give them grace and move on.
