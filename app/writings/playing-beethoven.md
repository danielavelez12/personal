The past month and a half, I’ve been preparing Beethoven’s appassionata sonata (3rd mvt.) for a recital. I got it to a state of memorization relatively quickly, but then I hit a wall. I kept spending hours daily practicing, but I couldn’t get the piece up to speed. Whenever I tried playing it faster, my fingers would trip over a set of notes, play notes unevenly, or wobble out of rhythm.

I realized the way I was playing Beethoven was completely off.

I’ve typically played late-romantic era composers like Rachmaninoff and Liszt, so my technique is optimized for legato phrases and expressiveness. This means, for example, putting my arm weight and wrist movements into my phrasing. My teacher would call this “pawing” the keys. By matching the dynamics with your physical movements, they come off as more natural. The goal here was to play lyrically, almost as if the piano was singing the melody line.

Beethoven’s sonatas have very different requirements.

1. There’s lyrical expressiveness here and there, but the beauty almost all comes through in the contrapuntal lines, recurring themes, and contrasting dynamics. You can play a Beethoven piece beautifully with robotic **precision and clarity**.
2. Beethoven’s appassionata sonata, specifically, requires **speed and fluidity**. The left hand plays a constant alternating sequence, for example, while the right hand zips through the main theme.

I went to a couple of coachings, and quickly realized what I was doing wrong. One of the coachings was a “fix-it session” that was livestreamed, and during it I was told — “this is the easiest fix-it session I’ve done!”

After I fixed my silly mistakes, my playing improved by 10x or more. I’d wake up to play after a previous day’s practice session, and see parts of my piece become buttery and smooth.

The essence of the fixes was simple. My fingers could play the piece fine, it was my arms and wrists getting in the way. In order to let my fingers do the playing, instead of my arms and wrists, I needed to do some thoughtful engineering of fingerings and positioning.

#### Engineering a technique for a passage

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!38QY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F604e8026-31df-4de5-8c14-e27adfe8cb13_522x164.png)

1. **Start with the end in mind:** for this passage of 16 notes, start with the last four. What is the easiest way to play those four notes? Record the fingering.
2. **Break down each passage into playable chunks:** go to the four notes before those last four. Can they be added easily? If yes, merge to chunk. If not, create new chunk, and re-analyze how to most easily play this new set of notes on their own.
3. **Glide hand in between chunks:** the way to unify chunks is not to twist your arm, or wrist, or do anything strange, because legato doesn’t matter. What matters is getting from one chunk to the other, with an easy glide.
4. **Review and optimize:** you need to make sure you’re not significantly changing your positioning from chunk to chunk, because there’s no time for this. Optimize for a positioning of your wrist and arm that will work well for the whole passage. This might involve iteration of fingerings as well, to make sure everything is feasible!

#### Practicing the technique

Whatever you do, don’t practice slowly. Practice at speed! This is the only way to ensure consistent practice — every time you play a set of notes, you play them the same way.

1. **Play each chunk on its own:** solidify the fingerings so that each set of notes becomes an atomic mental muscle movement.
2. **Play adjacent chunks together:** play parts AB, BC, CD, etc. it’s important to solidify transitions into the right fingerings.
3. **Play the whole passage:** solidify the whole set of transitions and fingerings together. You’re sewing together your muscle memory tapestry here.
4. **Play the whole passage with context:** play the passage with some of the part before, to train your muscles to set up in the right position to execute the passage correctly.

This practicing all requires conscious mental cues at first, then will all settle into muscle memory as long as the practice was consistent and clear.

#### Reflections

My learnings here have shaped my worldview — engineering matters so much more than execution. If you go in and just do things, you’ll fumble and do things inefficiently, and this will slow you down over time. Instead, you can unlock exponential performance gains if you prepare your execution ahead of time. What does this mean in practice?

- Writing to-do’s
- Setting up your physical space correctly (e.g. kitchen, work desk, etc.)
- Learning keyboard shortcuts and adopting tools
- Meditating and practicing positive self-talk

These are all real-life ways to prepare your mindset, environment, workflow, etc. so that you can execute flawlessly. Similar to piano, these involve mental effort, until they become habits.

Easier said than done, of course, so maybe I’ll need to play some more Beethoven before I can do all these well.
