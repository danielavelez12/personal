I believe that we shouldn’t describe consciousness as a binary state. Concepts like “I think therefore I am” make it seem like a conscious entity either exists or doesn’t. But it’s clear that all types of species do some level of thinking, whether it’s trees receiving neighborly communications through their roots or crows jotting down notes about the people they observe. If we established that all species of animals and plants are conscious, this wouldn’t be very helpful. The human species has evolved to have more complex brains than other species, with more neurons in the cerebral cortex, associated with advanced cognitive functions. If we agree that more neurons allow for a higher degree of consciousness, then we can ask what it means to have more or less of it. As we develop smarter AI agents, enabling them to approach human consciousness will make them more effective and better at coexisting with humans. Perfectly defining consciousness is impossible, since there’s so much unknown about how our brains function. But I gave some thought to building a practical definition that can be used to improve AI agents, or even work on being more conscious ourselves.

In order to define consciousness, we first have to define the space in which it exists. All living species use their brain to think and act in order to survive long enough to pass on their genetic material. Our brain takes in inputs through our senses and produces outputs that trigger reactions executed by our body. This process can be summarized in three actions:

1. **Detect**: to receive and store information from data collected by our senses
2. **Compute**: to process information and make instinctual decisions
3. **Execute**: to enqueue requests to our nervous system

Below is a chart showing these “category I actions” and their system dependencies. Storage is used to store information in the short term or long term, which the processor uses to compute outputs that are sent to a client-facing server.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UMX2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F99994551-2bde-4687-b674-57d68b800400_716x590.png)

The neural pathways for these actions are well developed across all living species. It’s the way our brains interact with our bodies and the world in order for us to do things like generate cells, produce hormones, or move our limbs.

Now, we know that these aren’t the only things our brain does. As humans, we make art, form relationships, and build cities. Since these aren’t critical for survival and reproduction, we can define a category of actions that are lower criticality and higher complexity. If we defined these from an alien’s perspective, they would all be described by one action: to mutate. We are always mutating physical objects, whether it’s our brain when we form a thought or a cup when we put it on the table. If we don’t touch something, we still mutate it, not in 3D but in 4D (its time coordinate is updated). Instead, I will use a first party perspective and describe these actions as ones that we deliberately choose to do by using a certain part of our brain (e.g. we don’t choose to mutate things in the time axis if we don’t think about them). We can define a set of these actions so that each action activates a certain set of neurons, and therefore our brains operate in a mutually exclusive way for each.

**Create / destroy / transform / maintain**

- To interact with entities (imaginary or physical)
- E.g. create an idea of a business, destroy a bad habit, transform a physical space, or maintain a plant

**Empathize**

- To form feelings
- E.g. being moved by the sight of nature or feeling sad for a friend

**Analyze**

- To form ideas
- E.g. determining the pros and cons of something or forming an opinion

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!A1BB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa87d9713-fe83-44b7-bc15-6393f14b6a00_800x609.png)

We could do multiple of these actions at a time; for example while I’m creating an idea of my dream house, I’m analyzing what makes a good home and empathizing with my past self about what I enjoyed in my previous homes. If we’re doing multiple at a time, we would see multiple groups of neurons firing together or one after the other. Another way to understand this is through role specialization. Each person typically does a combination of these actions repeatedly in their role, some more often than the others, depending on our job. For example, as an engineer I mostly analyze and create. A gardener maintains, a cleaner transforms, and a mother empathizes. We strengthen the neural connections corresponding to the action we’re doing most in our role, and therefore become relatively better at our role than others. These extend to other species as well. Bees, depending on their role in the colony, do a task such as maintenance, building, or taking care of the young.

If we add in the category I actions, we can imagine all these actions in a decision space, say as a set of concentric circles, representing two things:

1. Our brain can choose to do any of these 9 actions at any given moment.
2. The actions increase in complexity the further out they are.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!O5I7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Feea883d8-1ff4-4a10-8901-b7f6f3addd70_800x315.png)

Let’s also add in the concept of a “reward” that we reach by completing a set of actions. For example, Beaver Billy builds a dam using some quick maths and a bit of artistic energy, and receives the reward of feeling safe and sheltered.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N8Na!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2a494945-c793-4cbf-a853-f09be370978a_800x335.png)

Beavers instinctively know how to build dams, which means this mental pathway was optimized through evolution. Not only are beavers good at dam construction, but they also enjoy the mental and physical stimulation as well as the feelings afterwards. This makes for an activity that is instinctive, enjoyable, and satisfying.

However, humans do much more than beavers. They build tools, clothes, machines, and cities. How did evolution optimize the human brain to do this?

Consider a reinforcement algorithm that optimizes actions based on rewards. If it’s able to reach a reward sooner with an action, that action is then reinforced.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N52k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea782b99-5552-4f93-ac65-13f952837ad8_800x376.png)

Say you want the agent to reinforce an action (e.g. the action of building) despite an uncertain reward, however. The action would be reinforced slowly, since sometimes it results in a reward and sometimes it does not. Unless… you change the reward function for it to reach a reward deterministically.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-6xT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcfc84db2-fd88-4391-8f45-29001f4f24f6_800x345.png)

If you reward the agent upstream, at the action stage instead of the physical reward stage, then the agent will much more quickly reinforce that mental pathway. This development was advantageous for humans because they, more than other species, would try building random stuff, even with no real reward in mind. Through this random exploration, they learned how to build all the useful items that helped them survive.

But of course, it’s not like humans enjoy creating and beavers don’t. Beavers likely have a blast while creating dams. The difference is: you’ll ask Beaver Billy what he’s up to and he’ll say “building a dam,” while if you ask a human they’ll say “creating a home.” As highly conscious beings, we’re able to ***conceptualize*** our actions more. Even independent from the constraints of language, we’re able to simply recognize that we’re interacting with the world in a set of ways.

Once we understand our actions, we’re able to see ***purpose*** in them. We are willing and excited to do things just for the sake of doing them, even if they don’t show tangible results. There might be different reasons we enjoy them, such as impressing peers or designing something beautiful. Regardless, the core enabler here is the fact that we’re aware of our actions, and we‘re able to will ourselves to do them.

I believe, therefore, that consciousness is a measure of **how aware we are of our actions.**
