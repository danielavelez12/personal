There’s something about art that draws me in sometimes and repels me other times. I’ve always appreciated art that helps us notice the beauty of life; a painting that shows how the light hits a mountain, a piece that mixes rich dark tones with a light melody in a melancholy way, or a poem that speaks about the beautiful nature of humans. Even when it doesn’t have anything to say, it can just sit and be appreciated for itself.

But there’s something that unsettles me about art.

Art is supposed to be a universal human bond, yet some art is only accessible to those who speak its language. We’re born into a world that seems to have packaged gifts of beauty for us as humans to find along the way. Yet, some humans are unable to gain happiness from it, and suddenly we have a distinction between those that can appreciate art and those that can’t. And then inevitably, people go on to become skilled artists, and most of what they bring to the world can only truly be understood by those who have gone equally deep into the field.

It pretends to bring people together, but it really only creates a shared context removed from the truths of life. It generates conversations about the “aesthetics” of things rather than the things themselves, and deludes people into thinking they truly understand something, rather than simply being fascinated by it. In the end, people who are brought together through art may just have art in common but nothing else — is this sustainable?

Art is known as a benevolent force, but it is judgmental. Each art piece only serves those who are moved by it. A sentiment can only be appreciated by those who are already familiar with it, in which case it will resonate with that audience. If a person has not yet experienced a certain sorrow, they won’t be able to experience beauty like others do. Art judges its audience by how much life they have lived.

Not only does it judge, but it consumes. When it finds a worthy victim, it seizes them for an indefinite period of time into an indefinite depth. To listeners, it’s an immersion; to artists, it’s a drug. It removes people from reality and transports them into a space where nothing matters, until they are able to break the spell. It takes the souls of artists and demands sacrifice and pain for the sake of good art.

Regardless, it redeems itself in my eyes because it’s there when it is exactly what someone needs. It saves those who are most in need of saving from their demons and blesses those that are most seeking a love for life. It feeds souls thirsty for meaning and nourishes lonely bodies seeking warmth. It can be the addiction someone needs to feel content with life once it comes to an end, even if they’ve lost part of themselves on the way, and it can be the glue people need to live life together, even if it’s the only connection they share. The evils of art are necessary and are exactly what allow it to add meaning to humanity.
