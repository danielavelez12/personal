Caution: spoilers!

When I read the three body problem a couple years ago, I understood that: if aliens come to colonize Earth, we’ll be crippled by our societal divide more than by any technological gap. I just finished the first season of the show, which brought this societal rift to life for me. What was most interesting to me was the perspectives of these different sides. The scientists are the ones who care the most about saving the human species, while the non-scientists end up caring the least: they’re willing to instead surrender themselves to the Lord. In this world, the most rational are the ones who assign the most meaning to human life.

***Religious nihilism***

The masses who join the San-Ti army are deeply unsatisfied with the state of the world. They’re vulnerable and willing to seek salvation and purpose wherever they can find it. In the first scene where Tatiana appears, she lights Auggie’s cigarette and says, “it’s not easy, is it? Being a person in this fucked up world” (this is ironic, because the San-Ti’s world is the one that’s actually screwed up).

People like Tatiana are immediately drawn to the idea of being part of something greater than themselves. They buy into something like Pascal’s Wager: spend finite resources for potential infinite reward. One of the flaws in this philosophy is that it doesn’t specify which god to believe in, but in the San-Ti case, the god becomes real — the San-Ti promise salvation if you’re faithful. In the non-zero case that this is true, there is infinite reward. This is what the San-Ti army members are banking on.

I used to think that those that followed Pascal’s Wager were driven to it by its logical aspects. In the three body problem, though, the San-Ti army members are only believers because they have nothing else to believe in. They’ve lost hope in humanity, and can’t fathom finding potential infinite reward in anything else other than salvation by the San-Ti. Pascal’s Wager isn’t an optimal solution, it’s just an easy one.

The other flaw in Pascal’s Wager is that it doesn’t consider an evil god. If you believe in an evil god, then everything you care about might get destroyed: infinite penalty. However, this isn’t a consideration for the San-Ti army, because there’s nothing they care about enough — they’re essentially nihilists.

Behind religious extremism, I now see a form of nihilism. “nothing matters, except serving the Lord.” this could be seen in the attitude of Saul’s attacker when Saul confronted him in the hospital. Saul tried to reach him, to ask him why he did what he did. The attacker was lost in a haze, seeing nothing but his unwavering faith.

I grew up immersed in Catholicism, though thankfully a very progressive and tolerant form of it. Regardless, I sensed an intense constant need to surrender oneself to the Lord. In Catholicism, you’re reminded of your original sin and required to repent frequently, resulting in a deeply seated sense of guilt. I still remember mindlessly repeating in Spanish, “por mi culpa, por mi culpa, por mi gran culpa,” and wondering what we were taking responsibility for.

My Catholic upbringing helped me build traits I’m proud of, including my ability to prioritize others over myself. However, I’m realizing our Catholic guilt rings partially in nihilism. We’re able to completely dedicate ourselves to serving others because we lose sight of ourselves and any attachments that could get in the way. If the Catholic god appeared on Earth as the San-Ti, I don’t doubt that many devout Catholics would join the alien forces, not because they wish to do harm but because nothing matters except paying back their debts to the Lord.

***Rational existentialism***

Meanwhile, the rational characters in the show are the ones that ironically find the most meaning on their own. Their behaviors align with Jean-Paul Sartre’s existentialist ideology, “Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself.” each of the scientist characters create and follow their own internal compass of duty and righteousness. They are so emotionally bound to their sense of good and wrong, that they commit suicide upon discovering things that they can’t reconcile with.

Auggie and Jin end up going head to head because they have differing subjective ways of doing what’s right. Jin finds meaning in fighting for the future of the human species, while Auggie finds it in helping the underserved and improving their current quality of life. As scientists, they apply their rationality to defining their own moral guidelines.

Saul, who presents externally as a careless player, actually cares a lot under the surface. We see he pretends to forget his date’s name after sleeping with her, and relaxedly supports Will’s decisions yet can’t bare to see Will sign away his freedom at the end. Saul cares deeply about people.

And Will, the most nonchalant character in the show, deeply cares for Jin. He doesn’t care about much else, including the money he offers up to his family members when they pressure him for it. He explicitly refuses to promise loyalty to the human race and instead chooses to find meaning in his unrequited love for Jin.

Of course, this is a fictional show, but the director did a great job of showing how rationality can power nuanced, intentional existentialism. Personally, I’ve also found my brainy friends to be some of the best at building a thoughtful sense of meaning and committing themselves to it.

After watching the three body problem, I’m much more aware of how nihilism and existentialism can present themselves. In today’s society, it’s hard to tell people’s life perspectives because the decision stakes aren’t significantly high, leading to lack of conviction in decision making and/or guiding principles. When you flip the status quo, though, and present a default scenario where the human race will die, you can see people’s true natures and why they believe what they believe.
