Whenever someone asks me what my career would be if I wasn’t a tech startup nerd, if it could be something wildly different, I say I would be a professional pianist. Piano somehow weaves together all the core components I appreciate most of being human: expression, passion, personal growth, aesthetic, impact, soul, etc. I decided to live out my alternative career for a couple of months, December — January, and prepare a piece for the MIT concerto competition. Honestly, this was the first time I wasn’t just settling for mediocrity; this time I was hyper-focused on reaching perfection. This meant not only learning, but learning how to learn. Truly embracing “only perfect practice makes perfect.” Going so deeply into piano allowed me to push my limits and understand things I hadn’t been exposed to before.

It also gave me a lens to observe my brain through. Usually, we live in our own little minds in a world we construct for ourselves, and we say or do things just because we feel like it, but the feedback we receive in response to our decisions is convoluted and foggy. It’s hard to know which decisions were made from instinct, emotion, miscalculation, etc., and we rarely reflect on the circuitry of our brain, except for in certain cases such as trauma therapy in which we need to rewire our brains. In other cases, like learning material in classes, it’s a combination of classes, p-sets, or discussions that mold our brain, and we don’t really know what were the specific factors (timing? Content format? Cadence?) that led to our understanding of a concept.

When there’s a source of truth (the sheet music), and you can also interpret output directly (e.g. how something sounded musically), you can easily understand what was it that your brain produced at each point and why. You can detect exactly when you’re acting based off of emotion, or when you’re acting in response to a bias, or when you reasoned through a decision. Music as an output for the brain is especially interesting because it reveals the line between logic and emotion; a note can sound completely different if played as “I think this should sound this way” vs. “I want this to sound this way.” Music is like a debugging output with print statements everywhere for your brain.

When it comes to practicing piano, it essentially is the problem of optimizing brain rewiring, in a vacuum. Every note, every muscle action needs to be synced to the brain memory with the least noise possible. The naive solution is sitting down and memorizing each small thing separately, then their order; except there’s a multitude of ways this process can be sped up, mostly due to the intricacies themselves. Take, for example, the memory palace technique: it amplifies the data you have to memorize in order to optimize memorization. Weaving a tapestry of synapse connections takes strategy and understanding of the input space and your existing connections.

Once this tapestry is formed, this is the foundation for musicality and expression. Perfection is freedom in this way because once you have a language down perfectly, you can communicate anything you want. Most of the times though, this is directly involved in the learning process as well, the same way your learning of a spoken language is motivated, shaped, and enforced by what you want to be able to communicate.

This piece includes a few highlights from my learnings while attempting to reach perfection and musicality, with respect to some key parts of the 26 pages I lived in for a couple of months :)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-DtX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa741add9-fe3b-438c-88f2-724f3d9a1986_800x631.png)

The opening scene, grandiose and majestic, a single stream of notes that set the entire stage for the piece. Playing this part is like singing, with a special **focus on every note** and what its role is, whether it’s a part of the cascading waterfall, a sparkle at the top, or a suspenseful breath. “Con delicatezza,” it says, reminding me to place every note delicately on the table. I was able to pick up this part pretty easily not only because of the simplicity but mainly because my brain is always so *focused* during it — like walking through a path that you know like the back of your hand because you’ve given special love and appreciation to every beautiful tree along the way.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kPeB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6934f152-9c51-4b16-a420-dc5d364f74d0_800x571.png)

The piece quickly adds many, many more notes into the mix. As this happens, my mental focus draws away from the individual notes and loses its grasp. When I’m not able to think about each note individually, I focus on the **patterns I understand**. Every complex phrase reduces down to a set of sequences I’m familiar with from music theory. These sequences are not only mapped in my brain to labels but also to emotions, which allows me to characterize a phrase by a narrative-like series of sad, exciting, or joyful progressions, the same way we reduce large complex sequences of events to narratives and categorizations. These melodic narratives are made up of not only notes, but also rich dynamics and expression, so it’s actually more tough to play by memory just one hand or without any of the dynamics (e.g. all at the same volume).

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Jbag!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0584f595-989b-43a9-92e5-fe907158a9f7_800x582.png)

The piece is at full speed now, and waves of notes are tumbling down. The hardest parts of learning this were coordinating both hands and developing muscle memory I could rely on. Stretched out across the piano, my hands fight for attention from my eyes that I can’t give to both, and I have to strategize on where to look when. I learned I can’t just practice slow, because there are **distinct challenges that only surface at speed** and that require unique muscle movements. The most powerful practice came from practicing isolated muscle movements, at speed. Just one hand, or just a pair of notes, or just a sequence. Then, my brain wouldn’t be overwhelmed and could focus on developing each aspect deeply, until it could run everything together at scale effortlessly.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LdAI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10108eb4-6ec7-46e7-9ab9-e6e28bae9cd0_800x544.png)

This part taught me the **power of cognitive dissonance in incapacitating us**. In my practice, I kept losing precision on my right hand and misstepping into wrong notes. For hours I practiced both hands together, fooled by the red herring of the hand coordination problem with overlapping notes and diverging sequences. Then, my teacher asked me to play just the left hand, a seemingly simple task but which revealed that the problem wasn’t with my right hand or both together, but with my understanding of what the left hand had to do. I was using a left hand fingering that was nonintuitive and therefore was a confusing muscle action, in turn taking all my attention and disabling my precision in my right hand. Once I cleared up the confusion, the whole part together became perfect instantly. I deeply understood that cognitive dissonance surrounding a small, unexpected aspect can end up occupying all of our mental worker threads, leaving us to miss important information or make hasty decisions that we weren’t able to focus on. And most of the time, we don’t even realize this is happening until we isolate to identify which aspects we need to clarify for ourselves.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kfD8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1aae3c37-634e-4d32-8909-2dc743a37b6a_800x755.png)

The difficulty continues throughout the piece and the chords stretch out more across the piano, flowing more into melody and breaking out of predictable muscle patterns. While practicing this part, I realized the power of **intentionality and vision**, especially in the process of muscle development and habit building. My muscle memory for this part would degrade very quickly, vanishing after pretty much 1 hour in the early days and then weakening after a few days about a month in. Of course now that it’s drilled into my brain, I can play it in my sleep essentially, but while I was building this up, I had to rely on something more than just my instincts. I realized when I consciously imagined every chord a millisecond before playing it, I landed on it perfectly every time. Not an easy task given these chords come flying in, but worth it.

This learning contributed to my understanding of how important practice strategy is. You’re essentially figuring out how to **optimize for consistency of precision and accuracy**, because the most important thing for building muscle memory is landing on the notes *correctly,* not making mistakes and telling yourself, “wrong note!” Similar to the learnings of [Nicole Dieker](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uJunQdy3bvbk24noa/what-happened-when-i-applied-metrics-to-my-piano-practice-a) with her practice. I learned to appreciate this in the process of behavior change in general. While you’re building up a habit, you quite literally have to manifest yourself into doing it, until it’s instinctive. In the short-term, this means consciously willing yourself into starting a task even though it doesn’t feel fun at the moment, and in the long-term this means consistent reflection / meditation in order to envision yourself accomplishing the behaviors you want to adopt. The more you do things rightconsistently, the easier it’ll be to continue doing them.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!R-rw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb675a3fd-0edf-4c13-b4d6-26667e82fb9a_800x1048.png)

This part dissolves into a river of notes accompanying the orchestra, which has the melody shown in the bottom two lines of each bar. The tone completely shifts to one of wistful melancholy and desire, with streams of consciousness moving through in waves. The purpose of my notes is to wash over the scene delicately and smoothly, causing me to switch my focus from each individual precise note to a whole line of notes as a single flowing stream, rising up and down in volume and intensity, always just serving as a soft overlay for the orchestra melody.

This part came to me relatively naturally as I **immersed myself in the waves of emotion** contributing to the overall feeling of this part. Early on before I mastered the notes, I already led with a vision of deep musicality, which helped me frame my understanding of the notes and learn them more easily. The early, rough version of this part was a wireframe; I grasped quickly what peaks I had to get to and what chords to go through in order to match my vision, and as I practiced more, I simply filled in the missing notes more and more until I soon could play the lines of notes continuously. By subconsciously intertwining my rational understanding of the notes with an emotional record, my muscle-learning became intentional and purposeful. Similar to ideas such as those in the book “Descartes’ Error,” emotion was a critical component of my mental capacity and decision-making in this part, and this holds for many other things in life that we romanticize and attach meaning to.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wQAi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F949fe891-6cc0-408a-b439-d24bc72a377c_800x1087.png)

We go into a section that builds off of the last, with the same emotional intensity but now shifting into leading the melody, as the orchestra now plays accompaniment again. “*Molto espressivo”* prompts for intense expression throughout the interweaving melody lines, and my fingers here sing every note. Here, since I lead my own melody, I’m able to express myself fully through variations in pace and volume, racing up one melody line and slowing down another, or choosing my own hints of flavor to add in by emphasizing certain notes. By forming artistic opinions on all of these, I was able to more quickly understand and grasp this section, and I learned the power of **expressing myself while learning**. Pushing our brain to form independent thoughts, decide preferences, or make subjective judgements about a certain topic completely changes the way we process and retain the information. The notes, or components, go from just being present in a body of information, to serving a critical role in a perspective we hold about something in life, and therefore instantly become meaningful and important to us.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tvbL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faa8b3477-4998-42b8-85ef-a247c1b40b77_692x306.png)

A little later on, we go straight into this run of thirds, a straightforward pattern played by two hands, until we hit:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9cga!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F46848bf2-2443-4f73-b6c5-f5397ed69541_196x310.png)

And the glorious predictable pattern is broken, because now my left hand has to fly over to a lower octave instantly and doesn’t have time to contribute the last two notes of the run of thirds, so the right hand has to take those. This is probably one of the technical challenges that continues to haunt me the most. It’s a deep-seated mental pattern that has to be broken — my brain shortcuts through pattern matching and causes me to want to continue the run with the usual fingering, but I have had to teach my brain to contradict that at a specific point in the run.

The **key to this mental path rerouting is incremental practice.** I practice [i:x+1], where x is the mental pattern break and i starts off as something like x-3, and when I master that I practice [i-1:x+1], and so on. You would think that it suffices to practice just the mental pattern break, [x-1:x+1], and then append it to the rest of the run, since that’s how practice usually works: it works as a generally fixed width sliding window of [x:x+2] -> [x+1:x+3] -> [x+2:x+4]… that only transitions to the next sliding window once the current one has been practiced sufficiently. However, in this case, [AB] = “practiced” and [BC] = “practiced” does not imply [ABC] = “practiced.” If C is the pattern break and AB is part of the pattern, ABC will require more practice because of that added A that creates a longer, stickier trap into the mental pattern. Ideally we’d be able to place a marker on “B” so that our brain algorithm recognizes that it’s time to switch from the pattern soon, kind of like an alarm, but “B” can’t easily be distinguished; it’s just a part of the pattern.

In practical terms, this means that rewiring our mental shortcuts requires practice through an increasing number of context clues, since we can’t just put a mental note to act differently whenever X happens. If we want to stop instinctively turning left at a stoplight after a long familiar road, we need to consciously practice with the last part of the road right before the turn, then with a longer portion of the road, etc. until we can take the whole road and naturally take the right turn instead at the end. Bias rewriting takes dedicated awareness, practice, and discipline.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1LxV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d880d61-e978-4580-bc03-ed5432e1b2bc_516x298.png)

This part gave me a lot of trouble as well since it has various potential fingerings, and I made the mistake of accidentally switching between two different fingerings during practices and not fixing this, leading my brain to be eternally confused between two different finger patterns. I initially assumed on any given day, my brain would pick one fingering and go with that one, but instead, it would encounter decision paralysis. This taught me that in order to make quick, strong **decisions under pressure, it’s better to have a predefined decision framework** when we can. If sometimes we prioritize some personal values and other times others, we will fumble under pressure.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yo49!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F83deabea-0dc3-4905-86d2-9e839e0fb52a_800x671.png)

Even without fingering ambiguity, some parts of this piece didn’t make sense to me until a couple weeks later of practice. This part helped me understand the power of **letting things bake in the oven.** The accidentals and unexpected chords made these notes hard to mentally digest, and I originally could only play this part at about a third of the actual speed. After trying for two, three hour-long sessions, playing this part up to speed was a lost cause when I was in the early stages. However, every practice session would result in significant results a couple weeks later. I learned that if I study complexities slowly and meticulously, the aggregation of them won’t make sense in the moment, but it’ll become clear in a week or two, after my subconscious takes care of processing and maintenance.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QpZs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd4bd3395-3f87-40dc-af1f-391078306488_800x528.png)

This part was another constant struggle, which I was able to make easier for myself by choosing the right fingering. Throughout the last parts I’ve highlighted, the challenges mostly revolved around rewiring my brain, but this part was one in which I could initially optimize the fingering to significantly reduce the quantity of rewiring needed. This taught me the power of **utilizing our existing mental pathways whenever possible**. Specifically, the first line contains sets of right-hand sequences, each five notes (1 and 2 single notes, an intermediary pair of two notes, 4 and 5 single notes), so the key was setting my fingers up for success so that they would almost naturally fall into the right notes. An example of this is the suggested 251 fingering, which is unexpected but sets up the next three notes perfectly.

I’ve realized that in order to help myself remember a challenging concept or develop a new habit, it’s incredibly useful to front-load some mental effort in order to make the rest of the line of thinking fall naturally into place. This supports the power of [cross-domain thinking](https://www.cleanlanguage.co.uk/articles/articles/337/1/Applying-Cross-Domain-Thinking/Page1.html) as well as the memory palace, both ways to create some mental arrangement in order to make something easier for ourselves. In habit-building specifically, we can find “251 fingerings”, e.g. a hard, unfamiliar action that we need to spend some time practicing, but that sets us up for success for the rest of the sequence. For example, in order to shift my sleep schedule, I practiced one simple but challenging action: brushing my teeth as soon as I got home rather than before going to bed. This set me up to go to sleep more quickly later on since I would no longer procrastinate brushing my teeth. Just a little bit of rearrangement needed to streamline things for my lazy self.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eMU4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F72109c31-0420-44e3-a391-80a9a3f80e13_800x565.png)

We now flow into my favorite part of the piece, where there’s a sorrowful melody line brought out by the bottom notes in the treble clef played by the left hand. Similar to previous parts, emotion was critical in my process here. One of the key aspects of this part, though, is that the emotion line stretches across the four bars. It’s not only enough to appreciate every single note or group of notes for its beauty, but I have to consciously bring out the picture that the entire, longer line is painting, with long stretches of dynamics. This, again, served as a focus point that helped me learn the music better.

As humans, we are myopic in nature; we tend to only think about what’s in front of us. Practicing this piece of music taught me that a powerful way to combat that is to **romanticize a long-term vision** of what we wish to accomplish. This can have negative side effects if we solely use this tactic (e.g. idealizing a suboptimal decision or being driven by aesthetics and public image), so constant rational reflection is needed, but it is powerful for executing on a long-term plan. For myself, I often embrace my main-character syndrome and see my life as a movie in which I overcome whatever challenge comes my way and accomplish my dreams. Thinking about the high-level of what we’re doing and viewing it as something beautiful for ourselves can help align us and provide motivation for our day-to-day.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HST2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7ad99a38-d481-4d6b-af53-3d45caf6d80c_750x626.png)

The melody climaxes in two fanfare-like sequences, each ending in a triumphant chord. Throughout practice, I taught my right-hand to run across the sequence and catapult itself onto the last chord, ingraining this all in my muscle memory. I would practice intensely for a few days, then I wouldn’t come back to it for a while. After returning to it, it would be in differing states of accuracy (hitting majority correct notes, but with incorrect intervals) and precision (hitting majority correct intervals, but at the wrong notes). Most commonly, my hand would hit perfect intervals in the sequence, but slightly translated to completely wrong notes. I learned **we tend to remember the relations between things better than the things themselves.** Rather than focus on the specific notes my fingers should hit, I observed which interval was the weakest link in the chain, and I practiced just that interval, knowing that the rest of the sequence would follow with the rest of the intervals.

Learning a sequence in the form of a set of intervals is incredibly powerful; it turns a set of disparate datapoints into a linked list of enriched items. Intervals and chords, unlike notes, are linked to a much richer mental representation, each named (e.g. “minor third”) and correlated to an emotion-evoking sound (e.g. a sad chord). The linking aspect ensures that each interval anchors the next (AB -> BC), so we ensure correct notes by ensuring correct intervals. In a similar fashion, we can remember concepts by understanding the relations between each pair of concepts, spinning a robust knowledge web we can rely on.
