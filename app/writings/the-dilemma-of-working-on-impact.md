Growing up, I loved building random practical things like tissue dispensers and desk organizers (impact has to start somewhere). I competed at the state engineering fairs with a sheet music turner and a typing device for people with motor disabilities. I loved the process of 1) taking a problem people face daily and 2) adding some constraints - how do we do this with just these materials? Or how do we build something better than what’s out there? My problem-solving world expanded when I started going to Model UN conferences every other week, proposing solutions to vector-borne disease outbreaks or policies to govern space warfare. Coming up with solutions to real-world problems made me feel good about myself, and brought me an immense amount of energy. In practice, though, deploying and implementing these solutions is a different story. How do you work on problems that matter, while still letting work feel like play?

As a teenager, I received a tough lesson about the consequences of my actions. My grandpa was visiting us at our home in Florida, just recently cancer-free and typing away at his computer on his first almanac of agriculture. Recovering from jaw reconstruction, his mouth had trouble closing and would let out a stream of saliva every few minutes. I excitedly made him a mask that could hang under his chin, keeping his computer from getting wet, and he promptly wore it (as any good grandpa would). It rested under his chin for a few hours before my mom took it off of him and we realized it had rubbed off a critical piece of skin from his chin. His health tumbled downhill the following years; his chin never healed, he continued to be reliant on a stomach tube, and his immune system didn’t sustain him through multiple rounds of pneumonia, until he passed away while I was in college.

Fast forward a few years, I’ve now been working full-time in high stakes industries, and I still haven’t reconciled my love for building with my fear of hurting others. The day-to-day is an emotional rollercoaster. Helping build our cross-border transaction system was one of my favorite projects at Alza, supporting a user from the moment they submit the transaction until the cash is ready to pick up by their family member abroad. But I remember anxiously checking DataDog, hoping to see 200s on the cross border transaction endpoint, and feeling a jolt at seeing an occasional 500. The fear that things aren’t working often makes me less likely to check in on things. More recently at Tandem, a back-end feature I was proud of ended up causing an incident. During a retro, I reluctantly explained to my coworkers that the system bug could have caused patients to receive a medication that they shouldn’t be taking.

Apart from the anxiety that comes with shipping software in fintech or health tech, there’s also a component of cognitive dissonance. While working on my startup last year, building AI for banks, I was obsessed with the goal of expanding financial access and enabling instant capital for SMB owners, frustrated by the current funding gap and delays. But I constantly asked myself if software was the right solution. Would I just end up joining the myriad of predatory fintech startups enabling lending and credit at egregious rates? Did SMB owners want to lend from an AI agent, or from a human they can form a strong relationship with? I would often cringe at the thought of just throwing AI at a problem as large as the $5.2 trillion global funding gap.

Once you’re done fighting the internal mental battle of developing software to solve hard problems, you also have to show face for the users and stakeholders. At Alza, we worked on a couple of app redesigns to make our app feel more reliable and trustworthy. Fun colors turned to dark green and fonts became standardized. Internally at Tandem, we laugh about our webscraper bots and LLM-generated documents, while externally to our providers and enterprise clients, we present as a serious company who doesn’t joke around about patient-related systems. Features that bring users joy are deprioritized for engineering work that provides reliability and robustness. Users want their banks and healthcare systems to be functional, not fun.

The fundamental problem is: if you’re in a position to make a positive impact, you have the responsibility to avoid making a negative impact. If you want to support someone’s socioeconomic growth, you’ll have to be responsible for safeguarding their wealth. The closer you want to be to the average person’s health, the more harm you could make if things go wrong. With great power comes great responsibility.

The decision to work in fintech / healthcare seemed so easy when I was growing up - it felt like a dream to work on solving challenging problems that impact the average person. Now, I can see an identity crisis creeping in. Am I the right person to work in these spaces, or am I not careful and thoughtful enough? Will the heaviness of these industries dampen my optimistic spirit? Why must I choose in between having fun and doing meaningful work?

I haven’t fully staved off the identity crisis, but I’ve thought about a few practical solutions to my dilemma:

1. **Build sandboxes to play in.** I get joy from spinning up webscrapers, live iterating on front-end features, and watching orchestration flows process tasks. The more real it feels, the more exciting. This is all possible within the bounds of a development environment with proper data seeding and service mocking. The key is to invest in this so that you can quickly iterate and build things you’re proud of before even touching production. And an added side effect is you’ll lessen the anxiety of rolling out to production.
2. **Set up invisible guardrails and systems for yourself**. Humans aren’t machines, and if you’re like me, you’re more like a pinball bouncing between a hundred thoughts. I’ve learned to rely on a strong test suite, alerts, and project documentation and sheets outlining everything that needs to happen with a project. You can create room for spontaneous, unstructured work, if you mentally shelve off the process overhead. Tests that run in the background are manual tests you no longer had to run.
3. **Plan projects ahead of time.** I’ve learned to select projects ahead of time and pipeline them through the dependencies (design, product, infra, etc.) so that I typically have one project that’s ready for implementation that I can fully dive into. My naive self would love to start and finish projects day of, but relying on others will result in better outcomes. The second most-fun alternative is to fully clear a project, including last-mile considerations like design questions and edge cases, and then lock into a heads down building period.

The rest of the challenge is building up confidence. My goal is to *believe* I’m the type of person who can build great things and have fun while doing it. Step by step, I’m showing myself I can ship solutions that actually work for people and improve their quality of life. Regardless of the compliance and boring landing pages, there’s enough to love in fintech and healthcare that I’m willing to invest in my way of working in them.
