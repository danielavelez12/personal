Not very great work-life separation, and I usually try to not operate like a life-optimizing finance bro, but I’ve been thinking a lot about how to “underwrite” myself lately. How should you invest in yourself? How should you decide how much to invest in yourself?

I believe every person not only has a risk tolerance, but also an investment amount preference. There’s no need to sacrifice resources to invest in assets if you don’t care about the returns. I’ve learned that I personally need to be investing all my assets at any given point — whenever I have time, energy, or money just sitting around, I get antsy. What are the returns I’m missing out on by not spending all my resources? This gives me a good starting point for making my decisions, because I know withholding is not an option. The tricky part is knowing how to make these investment decisions.

**Build conviction through your identity.**

To invest requires conviction. It’s easy to have conviction in something or someone else, and attribute ultimate failure to external factors out of your control. When you invest in yourself, you have practically full control over the outcomes. You put your money where your mouth is, and you are forced to execute, or else 1) you failed on your investment decision or 2) you failed to execute. This is pretty scary. I’ve learned you have to build up trust with yourself by building an identity and sticking to it.

Early this year, I decided to early exercise all of my stock options for Alza. My conviction was partially based on the fact that I have control over the outcomes and I thought I could execute. The latter made it emotionally tough to make this decision; with power comes responsibility. If Alza failed, I’d have myself to blame. When we shut down, it definitely felt 10 times worse than if I hadn’t invested all my savings into Alza. However, I knew if I hadn’t invested fully, I would’ve been miserable during my time there.

I’m proud of my decision to stick around and stay until the very end. Even the last couple weeks that we were holding out for our last potential investors, I was pushing out new features and working on new projects. That gave me a story to tell and a movie to look back on. After we shut down, I spent days reflecting and writing on what I learned, so I could do better next time. All of this was also an exercise for myself: can I execute until the very end? Can I learn from my failures?

These sorts of exercises — taking a bet and then executing — are the best way to build conviction in yourself. It’s similar to how you might date someone before deciding to marry them. Do they show up in your life when they need to? You might trust them with small things, then larger things, in order to see how they fulfill expectations.

I’ve been pushing myself to do these sorts of exercises my whole life, but my primary motive was never to build a track record with myself. I’ve always preferred to finish what I start (books, projects, etc.) because of my core beliefs of who I am. I tell myself I am the type of person who follows through, which results in a positive reinforcement cycle. We hang on so tightly to our beliefs of who we are, that strengthening these beliefs is the most powerful way to motivate ourselves.

Once I left Alza and was deciding whether to go all in on my own startup, I had to remind myself of who I am. I decided on a whim to run a half-marathon while in SF, and I stuck to that. I did however much training I could do in a week, then carb-loaded and went to sleep at 9 pm the night before. The morning of the race, at 5 am, I couldn’t find my running shoes. I was so worried I’d miss the race and disappoint myself, that I searched my friend’s house (where I was crashing) and snagged some shoes I could, ehm, borrow temporarily. I ended up finishing the race 2 mins under my goal of 2 hrs. And then rushed home to return the shoes, of course. (I’m not a robber, I told myself!)

Aside from the marathon, I also was on my best game overall. I responded to friends’ texts, called my parents daily, and showed up for one of my high school friends who moved in to the city. I washed my sheets, meal prepped, and booked yearly bike and gym memberships. I started taking cold showers and called a friend to have a hard conversation.

All of these small actions led up to the big 24 hours I had to make a decision — to either join a company or start my own. So many negative thoughts swirled in my head. Investors and mentors telling me that I should wait until I have a unique idea or some more experience at a successful startup. Family pushing me to be measured with my risks and learn from others first. Friends warning me that startups aren’t easy. But I knew I was ready, and I took the bet on myself.

**Get to know yourself.**

One of the things that helped me make the decision to start something was to know myself well. I probably wouldn’t trust myself to skydive alone, because I know I have no practice opening parachutes, I hate falling, and I’m pretty forgetful. But I do know I can code and learn quickly, and I enjoy the 0–1 stage of building. I questioned myself early on in college: do I really want to be a founder? I took a few months to try my first startup full-time, and failed but learned I enjoyed every day of it. I learned what drives me and how to set myself up for success.

It’s easier said than done to know what you want. I still don’t know a lot about myself — I’m not sure that my favorite cuisine is Thai food, or that my favorite artist is Finneas, or that my favorite color is still teal. I kind of just said something the first time someone asked, and kept repeating my answer. How is everyone expected to know these things?

You can’t know everything, but you have to know the important things. And if you don’t know something, you can make a plan to figure it out. I had a set of questions I wanted to answer about myself, and it took me 5 years to answer them. I intentionally set out to try different career experiences and anything that I was curious about. I loosely held my beliefs about myself in order to iterate on them until now.

Sometimes I’ve felt like I knew myself, but I didn’t really. I’ve chosen books for myself that I had to trudge through and I’ve gotten into relationships that just made me feel empty inside. It’s easy to form an impression of yourself based on who you want to be, but not who you are. I’ve learned my insecurities are my blindspots. I’m terrible at memorizing people and places, making me a bad candidate for dense history books, but this is an insecurity of mine and I prefer to glamorize myself as a rising history intellectual, hence the misguided book selections. I believe people don’t change — you can work on your weaknesses, but they will always be your weaknesses and your strengths will be your strengths.

It’s hard to know when you know yourself well enough to take a bet. There’s always going to be some doubt. It can feel like stepping across river rocks— you mostly operate without thinking, one step after the other, trusting your instincts to find the dry, stable rocks, but one misstep and you lose all your balance unexpectedly. Can you trust yourself to continue crossing?

There were times in college when I felt that I couldn’t continue crossing the river. I had made some careless decisions that ended up hurting people close to me. I was so scared to make another move, to allow friends to trust me or to become close with people at all. Step by step, moving slowly at first, I clarified my understanding of myself. What are the situations in which I can be a good friend, or in which I won’t be able to? I learned how to set myself up to succeed as a friend, despite my weaknesses. Establish hypothesis, test, evaluate. Repeat. With positive results, I continue operating as if I know truth, until I might need to re-evaluate my hypothesis.

**Step by step. Eyes on the prize.**

The hardest part about investing fully in myself, so far, has been the immense pressure. I’m scared of letting other people down, but I’m even more scared about letting myself down. I gave myself full reign to try to build a successful company and somehow maintain my financial stability, and it’s time to show results. I’ve learned that you have to separate the investor from the operator in yourself, and once you invest, just operate.

The investor is cynical and risk-averse. They’ll try to find the potential downfalls in your path in order to hedge risk and set up backup plans. 1 in 10 startups succeed, so why will it be yours that does? What if it doesn’t? The investor in me diversified our investments a bit. I’ve continued my music hobby, signing up for my first NYC piano recital in October. Another big investment, for sure, but a completely different one from my startup. I’ve also kept my network warm, which will be a good safety net. These actions previously felt like a punch to myself. Why am I preparing for my failure? Do I not think I can execute? The key is to understand that we can have conviction in something, yet know that we don’t know everything. Let the investor in yourself set up the portfolio in a calculated way.

That being said, though, you have to go in big at some point. In poker, there’s a couple of traps. You might raise your bet by incremental amounts, allowing other people to stay for too long in the round, and then you’ll get knocked out by a surprise hand that became good by chance. Or you might just bet small amounts across the game and never win big with a good hand (or a good bluff). Either way, some of your bets have to be sizeable for you to beat out others and make significant gains.

Of course, there’s different bet sizes. You might take some smaller bets, then take larger ones, after you update your risk model and re-underwrite yourself. The important thing is to underwrite and commit. De-risk as much as possible, then just execute.

The way I’ve gone all in is to let the operator in me take control, no investor involved. I rejected job offers, blocked off my work calendar, and surrounded myself by the right mentors and founder friends to focus on my goal. I don’t think at all about the risks I’m taking or the opportunities I’m missing out on. There’s downsides to not constantly re-evaluating my investments, but it’s too much of a distraction to be re-calculating my route on the daily, weekly, or even monthly.

The uncertainty of startups, especially when the stakes are high, is intimidating. Being in control of the outcomes is still the scariest aspect for me. Skydiving with a guide turned out okay, while cliff-jumping on my own is paralyzing — how do I know what makes sense and what doesn’t? Should I dive into unknown voids that look rocky below? What if there’s something I’m not seeing?

What I do know is I have to take some jumps at some point, and I know how to gather the relevant data to make good *enough* decisions. I’ve underwritten myself based off of my current understanding of who I am and what I believe I can reasonably achieve, with a little bit of hustle magic. For now, I’ll be focusing in, and doing my best to do what I say I’ll do.
