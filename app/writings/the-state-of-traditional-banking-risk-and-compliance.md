We hear a lot about today’s neobanks, like SoFi and Chime, but only about [25%](https://www.pymnts.com/news/digital-banking/2023/only-25-percent-consumers-have-used-neobank-digital-bank-fintech/) of consumers have ever used a digital bank. Traditional brick-and-mortar banks continue to dominate the world of financial services and influence the day-to-day of the average American. Since leaving Alza, I’ve been diving into a fintech risk and compliance rabbit-hole and I’m curious about how it all looks at traditional banking institutions.

#### Why is there so much compliance?

Banks have to continuously adhere to multiple layers of compliance, both at the state and federal level. Regulating entities follow a set of motives:

**Maintain the stability of the banking system (especially after 2008).**

The **Federal Reserve** implements monetary policy that upholds a baseline reliability of our banking system. Every year, it conducts simulated stress tests to understand how our largest banks would fare in a downturn or a recession. It also ensures plenty of distribution of resources amongst banks according to the Bank Holding Company Act.

In order for our funds to be fully protected, banks and savings associations pay a premium to fund the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)**, which provides deposit insurance of 250K per depositor per institution. Specifically after 2008, the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act were created to further protect consumers, and are funded by Congress.

**Ensure that banks are adhering to regulations.**

The **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)** is the regulator that banks most interact with. It issues bank charters, conducts examinations, and can exert legal action if banks fail to comply. It’s not funded at all by Congress— national banks have to pay for their own examinations.

**Prevent money laundering and combat terrorism financing.**

Congress funds **FinCEN** to administer the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and USA PATRIOT ACT, which require banks to identify customers, report suspicious activity, and record-keep in order to prevent money laundering and combat terrorism financing.

**Make credit accessible, equitable, and fair.**

The Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) and Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) are enforced by federal agencies and evaluate banks based on their credit accessibility and lending practices. The **CFPB** helps enforce the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), which is the reason you can dispute your credit card transactions.

**Uphold transparency to consumers.**

Thanks to a bunch of regulations, banks have to add in those endless pages of terms and conditions. These include:

- Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z (overseen by the **CFPB**)
- Sarbanes-Oxley Act (overseen by the **SEC**)
- Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (overseen by various federal agencies)

#### What are the major risks that banks face?

- **Credit risk:** borrowers or counterparties fail to meet obligations
- **Market risk**: value lost due to changes in equities, interest rates, credit spreads, commodities, and FX
- **Operational risk**: internal or external fraud
- **Liquidity risk**: losses linked to market price drops
- **Compliance risk**: fines for non-compliance

Credit risk is the biggest risk for banks, but it’s also the risk that’s expected with the business model. Operational risk includes risk of fraud loss, which in the past year has occurred the [most](https://www.thebanker.com/US-consumers-lost-a-record-10bn-to-fraud-last-year-1709547619) with investment and imposter scams and through bank transfers and crypto.

#### How do banks manage risk currently?

Banks use a framework called an Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) framework, overseen by the Chief Risk Officer.

- Company culture, governance, and values
- Strategic planning, objectives, and goal setting
- Risk management cycle
- Monitoring and continuous improvement
- Transparency, communication, and reporting

#### What has been the risk and compliance trends in the past years?

**2020 anti-money-laundering law increases manual compliance**

The Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020, an overhaul of the Bank Secrecy Act, has been the most consequential legislation passed in decades, increasing manual compliance activities by [61%](https://www.americanbanker.com/list/5-trends-shaping-financial-crime-compliance-for-banks).

**[Increased](https://www.americanbanker.com/list/5-trends-shaping-financial-crime-compliance-for-banks) exposure to financial crimes of the following types:**

1. Involving cryptocurrency
2. Proceeds from human, wildlife, and drug trafficking
3. Use of money mule accounts to launder criminal proceeds

**[Less](https://www.americanbanker.com/list/5-trends-shaping-financial-crime-compliance-for-banks) time spent on customer due diligence since the pandemic, but still plenty**

Firms spend the longest time on large and foreign business accounts (58+ hours per business in a year) and foreign individuals (33 hours per individual in a year). These are approaching pre-pandemic levels, but not quite equal yet, since customers haven’t returned fully in-person to branches.

**Pressure on banks to claim accountability for fraud and provide more data to regulators**

- Pressure against large banks (e.g. Citi) to claim accountability for fraud cases
- Fed seeks more data about nonbank borrowers’ (e.g. private equity, mortgage companies, etc.) debt loads
- Regulatory compliance units grow (e.g. Chartwell Compliance)

**Losses due to investigations and fines**

- Federal investigation of AML controls lowers earnings (e.g. TD)
- SEC investigations cost banks 1.8B in fines due to lack of employee monitoring
