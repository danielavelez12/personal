### My last year at MIT ~

The first question asked at tech events, during meetups, or when you’re just catching up with a friend: “So what are you up to?”

Having been to way too many tech happy hours during my time in the bay, I programmed myself to give my spiel, a casual yet clean “personal narrative,” about what my passions are and how I’m currently chasing them through my internship, side projects, and/or club roles. When talking with a friend, I would be a little more vulnerable and talk about my challenges with those things. Regardless, the questions of “what my story is,” “how my life currently is,” and “who I am” all seemed to be answered by “what I’m up to.” On campus in college, I always took pride in juggling various endeavors that are all linked to my passions in some way, and I believed they all helped form my identity.

This semester, my answer to that question is now, “taking classes, some CS some math.” Of course, when prompted I’ll go into a passionate rant about how cool I think my classes are, but my answer is simple, and it is no longer a winding trail that wraps around every activity I currently do and how it ties into my personal story. What has changed is that my identity no longer is based solely on the current state of what I’m working on, or even just based on my work at all. I’ll dive into this, but first I wanted to share a passage from “Goodbye, again” by Jonny Sun that really resonated with me:

This passage reminded me of so many feelings: fear of failure, yearning for an identity, coping in response to a society that pressures us to produce constantly. And it also helped me recognize the pressure I felt to have so many “things” I was doing at once. I was building up my tetris board, and my incomplete rows were my identity at any given moment.

This semester, I decided to step down from my club exec roles, research, and side projects, and just focus on classes and friends. At first, this was a huge relief and weight taken off my back — I finally had a break from relying on other people and having other people rely on me. Soon after, it felt like I had lost myself. I no longer was helping build the entrepreneurship community on campus or hacking on cool stuff on the side. I felt like a fake — how could I say I love doing these things if I wasn’t doing them on a daily basis? My identity turned into a blank screen, with no blocks on it at the moment.

As a result of reducing my work commitments, a couple things started happening. Firstly, I found I had consistently recurring free time to do things outside of work; play chess, go on city adventures, read books, spend time with family, and practice piano. Secondly, I’ve been able to fully appreciate my classes and what I’m learning. With school being my only work commitment, I’ve constantly been “in the zone,” finding energy to work on problem set after problem set without my mind thinking about the next productive thing to switch to. I’ve switched from jack of all trades to attempting to do one thing really well. Instead of taking pride in all the things I do, I take pride in pushing myself in my classes, being curious about subjects, improving in my hobbies, and being a kind person with my friends and family. All things that don’t depend on my work, but on my values and how I’m pursuing them constantly. What matters is *how* and *why* I do the things I do.

Though my identity largely relies on my values, it continues to involve my work, when it comes to accomplishing my life goal of impacting society in a positive way. I’ve rebuilt my sense of identity around what I do. It is now formed on a grander scale, and not solely based on what it is at any given moment. For some part of my life I’ll be working in fintech to empower people financially, and maybe for another part of my life I’ll be a founder in that space, and maybe during another part I’ll be out of software engineering and/or tech roles. And some of these things may overlap, but they don’t need to be happening at any given moment or simultaneously. Focusing on classes this semester, I’ve learned to fully immerse myself in what I’m doing at the moment, without worrying about if my identity is “incomplete,” without worrying if it is exactly the *thing* I want to do in my lifetime.

This is not the common mindset; usually, people preach, “if you want to do something, then do it now.” I agree with this mindset, in the sense that (if given the privilege) you should always try to do what you love and you shouldn’t hesitate chase your dreams. For many people with similar goals to mine, this means dropping out of school or becoming a founder as soon as possible. For me personally, this means: have a plan to accomplish what you want to do, and enjoy the opportunities you’re given in the meantime. I know I’ll start a startup at some point, and I know I could try doing it full-time now if I wanted to. However, I am currently something I love — learning, meeting brilliant people, and growing, and it’s an opportunity I’ll never have again. Especially in tech and startups, careers are circuitous. I’m excited to continue leading life by diving into what I’m compelled by, committing fully to what I’m doing at any given moment, as long as I love what I’m doing and it helps me grow.

In the future, my identity might fluctuate and start to merge more and more with my career, depending on how engrossed I am. For now, when I’m explaining who I am to people, I’ll definitely mention some of the projects I’ve done or plan to do, but mostly, I’ll talk about things like: 1) why I’m obsessed with dogs and how I stare at strangers’ pets, 2) my favorite book and why it impacted me, and maybe 3) what my family’s farms are like in Colombia.

So, I’ve thankfully moved on from the “tetris board.” My identity is a canvas. With a layer of paint I sloppily added 10 years ago and some splatter from over the years. A cat walked over it and left some interesting artwork. And then it has some shapes I’ve outlined, and then some I’ve filled in. When some of the painting dries, I paint over it to make it richer, or I redo it completely, or I leave it there for eternity because it’s my favorite part of the painting. And then some glitter and corgi stickers. It keeps every layer, and it is always a work in progress, one that I’m proud of.
