The other day at Tandem we did a deep dive into healthcare land to learn about the flow of money behind medication access. It blew my mind that one unit of medication can cost up to $10,000 - even though its actual worth can be less than $5,000.

**In an ideal world…**

Manufacturers would set prices low in order to increase demand amongst patients and prevail against competitors. Furthermore, if prices increase, premiums would increase, and employers would switch to a different insurance agency. Upstream, the insurance agency would now be pressured to choose a PBM that offers lower cost medications so that their clients don’t churn. And so on, adding pressure from the PBM to the manufacturers to lower prices back down.

**Why we can’t have nice things**

The first problem is that there is little competition in the world of specialty drugs or biologics. Most drugs enjoy [12-15 years of market exclusivity](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/the-economics-of-the-pharmaceutical-sector-innovation-competition-and-patent-policy/) after approval, during which manufacturers operate as monopolies. Manufacturers will even do some “evergreening” - adding minor modifications to extend the patent life - in order to preserve their market position.

The second is that demand doesn’t really drop with high prices - medications are often price inelastic. In the face of skyrocketing costs, patients will just [go into debt](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-burden-of-medical-debt-in-the-united-states/) paying for their treatments and medications. For employers, the switching costs are too high to consider changing insurers for lower premiums.

And finally, there’s the **rebate**. Manufacturers want to secure [favorable formulary placement](https://arc.net/l/quote/pypudhvb) with PBMs, so they’ll incentivize PBMs with hefty rebates. Instead of seeking lower medication prices, PBMs will actually seek higher medication prices in order to receive a larger rebate (since it’s often a percentage of the medication cost). They can do this because they have massive dominance consolidated amongst the three of them (CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, and OptumRx), so they don’t worry about payers complaining about high medication costs. Even more, many insurers now **own** PBMs (e.g. UnitedHealth owns OptumRx), so they’ll actually benefit from the rebate.

**There’s hope**

This is one of the bipartisan problems that unites our country. Public demand is driving government negotiation of drug prices, caps on price increases, and greater transparency in pricing practices. With AI drug discovery and outcome prediction, we could see prices go down as a function of reduced research expenditure. Currently, [90% of drugs](https://averyfairbank.com/using-ai-for-drug-discovery-could-cut-costs-by-as-much-as-70-is-ai-worth-the-hype/) fail during clinical trials, and AI could help identify risks before advancing a drug into costly human trials. With all this, we might see increased efficiency, and one day the U.S. might start spending less than [14,570](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/historical) per person on healthcare.
