My co-founder and I have been putting on our sales hats for the past couple of months, selling to investors and potential enterprise customers. We’ve fully gone through the sales cycle with a couple of customers, from initial calls, to demos, to sending over a paid contract for them to sign.

My brain has gradually been rewired to have a sales mindset, and the biggest shift has been the way I think about the “asks” I make. They need to be direct, easy to say yes to, and honestly, presumptuous.

The way I used to operate was to believe that if someone else needs something, they’ll put in effort to achieve it. This means they’ll put in effort to schedule a call, or check out our product, or let me know that they’re interested. I also tend to operate with low-ego, so I prefer to let the other person decide whether my offering is the right for them or not.

I realized this is doing people a disservice. People typically don’t know what they need, and even if they do, they often don’t put in the effort to achieve it. That means if you think you have what they need, they *want* you to hard-sell it to them and make the sales process easy for them.

The “ask” is the point at which you prompt action from the other person, moving them further down the sales pipeline.

With my old mindset, my asks use to be:

**[I offer X.] [Do you need X?]**

- Here’s what we’re building. Let me know if you’re interested in learning more!
- This was a great conversation. Would love to chat again next week, but no pressure if you’re busy that week.

Very lukewarm and frustratingly indirect, and they put a large onus on the other person. They now have to:

1. Make a move and suggest a next step (e.g. “I’m interested, what time / day works for you?”)
2. Think through whether they even want to make a move (e.g. evaluate you and your offering and whether they want to move forward or not)

It’s your job as a salesperson to know the customer well enough to already know that they need what you’re offering. You’ve already done the “qualification” work, so the customer doesn’t need to. The qualification work here entails 1) understanding the customer’s needs 2) deciding whether the offering can solve the customer’s needs.

In fact, leaving the qualification work to the customer is unexpected, and might make them think that there isn’t a fit. If they’re being told “no pressure if you’re busy,” they’ll understand that their other commitments are higher priority. Everyone is busy, but they want to hear that your offering is worth their time.

My asks have now become:

**[I have something you need.] [Are you ok with proceeding?]**

- Would love to share how our product could help your org do X. Are you available tomorrow at 4 pm for a quick call?
- This was a great conversation. Does next week same day / time work for a follow-up?
- Will send over a contract. What contract timeline works for you?

These asks already assume that you’re moving further down the pipeline, and just prompt the minimal amount of action and consent needed from the other person.

This is a delight for the other person because they can feel at peace that you’ve done your due diligence and qualification. You’re fully invested into the relationship and expecting that it’ll produce value on both sides. Even if they’re not sure that they’ll pay for the offering, they can entertain the proposal knowing it likely won’t be a waste of their time.

I’ve been reading Founding Sales by Peter Kazanjy, and it describes the sales mindset as unwaveringly optimistic. You have to believe that every potential customer either 1) needs your offering now 2) will need it in the future 3) will go with a competitor. Therefore, you should assume that the customer should move forward with you, and you’re just asking them as a courtesy!

Of course, in order to get to these assumptions, you need to know your customer well. That involves getting as much information as possible, as early as possible. What does their org look like? What are their workflows? What are their company goals? Customers like to talk about themselves, and it’ll make them trust you more because they’ll feel understood. If you go straight into [I have something you need] without asking questions first, the other person will think “you don’t know me!”

Reflecting on other areas of life, I think the sales mindset is critical to helping create any value. I recently went on a couple of dates with someone who had pretty good rizz (relationship sales).

1. Right after first date, we exchange texts about how we enjoyed the first date. (Relieving the burden of qualification for the other, signaling that there’s a fit)
2. Couple days later, I get a text — “Ice skating on Thursday night at Rockefeller?” (Ask that’s so easy to say yes to)
3. On the second date, he surprises me with a couple of Danimals yogurts, based on my childhood nickname. (Details that remind me that he understands me)

People say in relationships you have to seem “not too interested,” but I think that’s false. The mindset you should have is “I have lots of options, but I’m very interested in you.” and the way to communicate that is to intentionally push the relationship forward and make asks that are direct and easy to say yes to.

Sales can be an act of love, not just romantically but also in friendships:

- Friend group plans. Do the thinking — put together a plan that you know your friends will be excited about, and choose a time / place. And then, just ask “who’s in?”, or better even, send them a calendar invite also! No shame in being a calendar nerd. The important thing here is to take responsibility on crafting a worthwhile time for you and your friends, they’re trusting you on it.
- A simple day-to-day favor. A friend is looking for an apartment, and you came across a place available. Sell it to them! Research it and see if it fits your friend’s needs, then tell your friend exactly why they might like it. If it’s easy for them to say yes to, and they end up loving it, they will thank you.
- Advice to a friend. Your friend is stuck in a job they’re unhappy in, and you’ve come across a different job that you know they’d thrive in. Pitch them on it — it could be the push they need to change their life for the better.

If you spend time qualifying an offering for someone, this is a gift you can give them. Not only are you helping realize value for them by dedicating mental bandwidth, but you’re also offering to be partially liable for the decision. It’s easier for them to say yes to this (people don’t want to be fully responsible for their own potential mistakes!).

To be a good salesperson, you have to have an ability to empathize with someone and imagine the future. You have to understand people’s needs, visualize how an offering will improve their life, and communicate that to them. And if you can do this, you have the power to help improve the lives of others whenever, whether it’s customers, or your own loved ones.
