Note: Mostly a hypothetical thought experiment written from my own perspective.

It feels so common now to go through a checkout process and check an additional box for insurance. Yesterday I booked bus tickets, added on the $9 insurance coverage, and received a follow-up email from Allianz Global Assistance, a company that manages [hundreds of billions of dollars](https://www.allianz.com/en/about-us/company/at-a-glance.html#:~:text=About%20Allianz,-The%20Allianz%20Group&text=Allianz%20is%20one%20of%20the%20world's%20largest%20investors%2C%20managing%20around,**%20of%20third%2Dparty%20assets.) in assets. We’re paying more and more for general insurance like healthcare and life insurance - the U.S. has seen a [3-fold](https://www.atlas-mag.net/en/article/the-american-insurance-market-the-world-s-number-one-market) increase in insurance premiums in the past 20 years.

**Is insurance worth it, on principle?**

I see insurance as purchase of stability: as a consumer, you’re paying X in order to avoid losing Y with a chance of X / Y (assuming a perfect, break-even insurance model). On the surface, it seems logical to purchase insurance, with the conditions that:

1. You’re paying a fair premium - e.g. you’re not a low-risk policyholder paying the same premium as the rest of the insurance pool)
2. There’s no moral hazard for yourself - e.g. you’re not incentivized to make choices that’ll result in you being worse off

The reason to purchase insurance is so that you can have more steady cash out/in flow and be able to make riskier or larger investments without worrying about liquidity. As an individual, your tail risk costs will generally represent a larger portion of your assets, but a small percentage of the assets an insurer manages, so it makes sense to outsource payout liability to them. Insurers pool risk across millions of clients and time horizons, helping them provide significant value with fair premiums.

**For example, on average, are healthcare and auto insurance worth it?**

An initial stat: If a U.S. consumer has both full coverage auto insurance and private health insurance, their total average cost would be about 10K. A couple gut checks here:

- Paying $2.5K on auto insurance is reasonable for costs that can [range](https://smartfinancial.com/car-accident-cost) from $5K to $60K. Some auto insurance policies will cover [up to $300K](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/9-compare-prem/auto-limits.cfm) in costs for injuries or fatalities.
- Paying $7.5K on private healthcare insurance makes sense given the average person spends [$11K](https://www.hipaajournal.com/personal-healthcare-spending-in-the-united-states/) on healthcare per year (breakdown below).

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!urKX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F070d51c3-a007-408e-a179-97880b8c2df9_733x377.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!urKX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F070d51c3-a007-408e-a179-97880b8c2df9_733x377.png)

For both healthcare and auto insurance, the coverage events are enough out of our control that it *seems* to make sense to buy insurance here rather than try to lower the event likelihood. Illness and car accidents can strike for anyone at any time.

**But if we fully exert our ability to mitigate the event, is insurance still worth it?**

Quick naive thought experiment. Assume I (a thankfully healthy 24-year old female living in NYC) pay [$4.2K](https://smartfinancial.com/average-health-insurance-costs-by-age) per year for a silver plan that includes everything from emergency services, to lab services, and even basic preventative services.

How could I instead spend this money to lower my chance of illness and/or injury, in ways that aren’t covered by my insurance? Routine wellness visits and screenings are still important, so I’ll keep those in for $500. This leaves me with $10 per day. Some options:

1. Uber every few days instead of biking
2. Maximize my wellness experience - Equinox or Bathhouse
3. Splurge on a health-nut lifestyle - Oura ring (~$400), monthly hiking trips ($600), 5 Lululemon fits ($1K), and a couple more granola girl trips abroad ($2K)
4. An extra $3 per meal in additional health value
5. An extra $300 per month in rent to live somewhere safer

These each sound great, but there’ll still be a good chance of infectious disease or accidents, resulting in $1.5K in an emergency visit and $1K in medication. This isn’t that much to set aside vs. invest - if I put it in a savings account rather than invest it, I might lose out on around $125 in a year. And maybe, I could instead gather 10 health-minded friends to pool risk together at a yearly cost of $250 (pp) to cover an emergency that has a 1/10 chance of occurring. In general, each of us would get the rest of the $3-4K to spend on lifestyle improvements and healthy decisions, instead of healthcare insurance premiums.

There is still the small chance of something catastrophic occurring, and those bills could add up to hundreds of thousands of dollars. So going uninsured is definitely a gamble - but I personally wonder if I wouldn’t mind taking on a 10K bill for a friend’s cancer treatment. (Not financial advice.)

**So how should this affect my insurance purchasing decisions?**

Though insurance still helps cap massive downside and bring peace of mind, my brief rabbithole has led me to believe it’s easy to overpay for insurance. Two thoughts:

1. **The average person will fail to take preventative actions** - possibly because of our typical human mindset or external limitations. The fact that average healthcare costs for a person like me are so high makes me believe that 24-year old women aren’t making all the preventative choices they could make. For things like car insurance, I’d easily believe that the average car owner favors speeding over minimizing their chance of accident.
2. **Preventative actions are hard to measure**, and therefore can’t be covered by insurance (yet). For me, e-biking is probably the most dangerous thing I do on a daily basis, which is something that apartment location and ubers could fix, but wouldn’t be covered. Not even my bike helmet is covered. (Maybe I should just stop e-biking anyways. Will report back.)

In certain cases, I purchase insurance because I know I’m in the riskier side of the pool, such as cancellation insurance for trips that I’m iffy on. But for other insurance decisions, I’ll be asking myself whether it makes sense to put premium payment towards preventative methods instead.

Anyways, if my friends and I ever get taken off our employer health insurance plans, I’ll be ready to start up a homies insurance club. Whoever falls off the self-care wagon owes us dinner.
