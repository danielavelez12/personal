In the past few months I’ve been captivated by a few startup books:

- **Bad Blood** (by John Carreyrou, about Elizabeth Holmes, Theranos)
- **Careless People** (Sarah Wynn-Williams, Meta)
- **Shoe Dog** (Phil Knight, Nike)

Looking back, I realized I unintentionally got to see wildly different perspectives of the startup journey. Each person had a completely different relationship with the startup industry. Elizabeth defrauded everyone, Sarah was done wrong, and Phil was mostly chilling.

The driver for each of their relationships was their background - how much familiarity and leverage they came in with.

Sarah was entering the tech world from New Zealand, half across the globe. She was in talks with Meta for a job for a year before she was able to secure an interview, and she didn’t even consider negotiating since the team was already so hesitant to hire her for the nontraditional policy role she was pitching. Years into her time at Meta, one of her fellow execs, Joel, reminded her coldly that she was “different” - not a Jewish Ivy League grad like all the others. What stood out to me was her uphill battle as an outsider to become a trusted peer.

Meanwhile, Elizabeth grew up right in Palo Alto, childhood friends with Tim Draper’s daughter. Elizabeth’s investors were power brokers like Henry Kissinger and George Shultz, who treated her as a granddaughter, hosting her for tea in their homes. Her first hires were Stanford labmates and throughout Theranos she hired heavily from her personal network. It doesn’t surprise me that she was able to get away with so much fraud, when she had a halo effect of trust from everyone around her.

Elizabeth spoke the Valley’s language and knew how to project certainty in rooms that rarely questioned her. When the cracks in Theranos began to show, she wasn’t met with scrutiny, but loyalty.

Phil wasn’t as embedded into the startup ecosystem from childhood as Elizabeth was, but he had his good amount of serendipity. He became co-founders with his track and field coach, Bill Bowerman, who was in the national top 10 and trained 31 Olympic athletes. Bill asked him if he could join him as business partner even before Phil thought to ask. Nike was founded in the 1970s, before venture capital became widespread, but Phil had a good relationship with his local banker, who also knew his dad well. In the first 10 years of Phil’s company, he was able to take out loans or raise money from friends multiple times without much pushback.

Phil could’ve played up his pitch and painted Nike to be something it wasn’t, like Elizabeth did with Theranos, but he didn’t have to. He was building a product he knew in and out, and with much less scientific risk! Elizabeth based her company off of a paper she wrote during her freshman year spring break, and when it came time to execute on her pharmacy partnerships, she felt forced to start taking shortcuts, and her position in the industry allowed her to.

I didn’t expect these books to speak to each other, but they did. They told a collective story about how tech isn’t just a meritocracy, it’s an ecosystem built on access, trust, and the stories people are willing to believe.
