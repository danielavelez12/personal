I just finished listening to Abundance by Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson and I’ve never resonated so much with a set of perspectives. Growing up and back in college, I lived in a mode of scarcity - thinking life is a zero-sum game, feeling afraid to spend energy and money on non-critical things, and limiting my focus to a few top priorities at a time. At new years, instead of writing new year’s resolutions I would write a list of things I needed to cut from my life. My first year of adulthood, I realized this was closing me off from a world of possibilities and placing an artificial cap on my bandwidth. Since then, I’ve grown into a mindset of abundance. Given endless resources and energy, what do I want to do with my time?

I’ve also started to think about organizations and systems in this way. At work, it’s easy to say we want to cut costs and focus on maintaining high product and service quality. What’s harder, but 10x more effective, is imagining how we could do things differently and what big bets we could be taking that we’re not taking currently.

Abundance is a book about how the U.S. government has adopted a mindset of scarcity, causing us to limit our climate efforts, housing, tech innovation, and more. I resonated wholeheartedly with Derek and Ezra’s take that the U.S. government could, and should be doing more. Not only by focusing on a wider variety of efforts, but also by embracing change and adaptability.

A few ideas that stood out to me:

#### ***“the difficulties of deprivation have been joined by diseases of affluence”***

I used to correlate “affluence” with “abundance,” but now I see them as opposite forces. As homeowners care more about maintaining their home’s value, they fight harder to keep multi-family homes out of their neighborhoods. Real estate becomes valuable only when it’s increasingly scarce. This means in order to work towards accessible housing, we have to offer ways for Americans to accrue wealth other than through their property. And this is where the government has to step in and policy decisions that may be unpopular with the deep pockets in the short-term.

Another problem highlighted is the growing regulatory burden on construction in the U.S. To build a km of a tunnel in the U.S., it costs us **609 million**, while Portugal builds a km at **96 million**. (And Portugal has even more worker unions than us.) I see this as another disease of affluence - our wealthy cities have piled on process and regulation, instead of prioritizing city development. About half of government employees have a background as lawyers in the U.S., resulting in adversarial dynamics that limit progress.

#### ***“think of government tech as layers of sediment. as new problems emerge, new layers are added, but the old ones are rarely removed.”***

After working on my startup last year in the KYC / KYB software space, it was a bit satisfying to learn that U.S. government with this area of tooling the most. They’re relying on a stale enhanced due diligence (EDD) system that they built in Cobalt, and they keep evolving the existing system instead of rebuilding it. The government is overloaded by tech debt. It seems like everyone knows that government tech needs some revamping, but no one wants to lead the effort to scrap the existing systems and build anew. Instead, they’re outsourcing their new tech initiatives to contractors like Palantir. I wonder though, how much better government technology could be if it was developed and maintained in-house, closer to the policy and decision makers.

#### ***“ideas aren’t getting harder to find, they’re getting harder to use”***

All major inventions required investment not only at the research and development phase, but also through product and market innovation. Alexander Fleming published his findings on penicillin in 1928, but for years no one figured out how to produce it in useful quantities. In the 1940s, the U.S. launched a massive program to help distribute penicillin, and they were able to give penicillin to every Allied soldier on the battlefield in 1944. The book explained how the U.S. not only could support ideas more at the research phase, but could also invest much more in helping make them useful.

In general, the book also explained how the government should be a more “active agent of invention.” We don’t have to wait for a war or another pandemic in order to bring forth new technologies. The government can just declare a crisis, depending on what they deem critical to work on. If they can’t fund the development upfront, they can offer an advanced purchase agreement, something they’ve done in the past for things like new vaccines.

I believe the possibilities are endless for how the government could support innovation. They have the liberty of not having to optimize for business profit or growth, but for human progress and societal development. I think Trump’s tariffs, apart from being economically questionable, should’ve been combined with robust efforts to boost supply through domestic industry innovation and expansion. We’re at a critical point where we both need to solve existential risks and have the opportunity to 10x human progress through new technologies, and a shift in government focus and attitude could make all the difference.
