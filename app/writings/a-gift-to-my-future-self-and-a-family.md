I was able to work out today for the first time after a few weeks of pills, injections, and my first post-surgery recovery period. I’m still unable to fit into my jeans and worried about falling asleep at my desk from exhaustion. But I did get a bouquet of pink roses in the mail, with a card that felt like the hug I needed.

I’m officially an egg donor to Rina and Rishi and proud parent of 12 frozen eggs. Thanks to Lauren Makler and Halle Tecco, the two crazy impressive co-founders of [Cofertility](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/12/cofertility-lets-women-freeze-their-eggs-for-free-through-its-donor-matching-program/), I was able to freeze my eggs at no cost and donate half in the same cycle. I’m hoping I made it in as a datapoint towards their 7.25M Series A raise this year.

My journey spanned over a year, starting in last July.

**The genesis of the idea (5/27)**

Last summer, while considering embarking on a startup of my own, I was deep in life-planning mode. Committing myself to building a company for 10+ years was a daunting decision. I’d have to deprioritize a lot of things for the next 10 years, including motherhood - I was doubtful I’d be a great first-time mother and early-stage founder at the same time. So, kids might have to wait.

During one of my instagram scrolls, I stumbled upon a Cofertility ad to freeze my eggs at no cost. Egg freezing is something I had considered, but only as an employee or VC portfolio company benefit. Paying 10-15k per attempt was out of the picture as I was trying to preserve founder runway.

What drew me in was Cofertility’s messaging - the warm, gentle invitation to help a family who can’t otherwise conceive. It felt so much more natural than all the ads I had been receiving to sell my eggs as an “educated woman”, valued highly in the marketplace. I knew how much I want to start a family at some point, and I beamed at the opportunity to help someone else with the same yearning.

I entered my information for an intro call, though had already set my mind on seeing this through. I knew it would lift a weight off of my shoulders. I’d get 10 years with better chances of still having children afterwards, and I’d be able to give someone a life-changing gift.

**The matching process (6/17 - 7/23)**

After an initial application and intro call with Coco, an ops manager at Cofertility, I was accepted into Cofertility’s Split program and sent a profile to fill out. It felt like a combination between a fellowship application and a dating profile. I uploaded my proudest pictures (the type I send to my mom) and chubby cheek baby photos. And then, I spent too long answering each and every profile question - hobbies, talents, life goals, strengths, ideal “perfect” day. The Cofertility team got back to me and asked for revisions, after which I made my profile even more compelling. There was an additional questionnaire to understand my tastes, including things like my favorite fruits. My photos, fact lists, and short essays were then live for candidate parents to browse through.

On July 10th, my member advocate, Erin, emailed me with an email titled “You have a match!”. I then met with Erin and learned for the first time about the intended parents, Rina and Rishi. I’d soon have the chance to call with them and decide if I wanted them to be my donation recipients, but they sounded lovely already. On this call, I also set my future communication preferences with their future child. There were various options, including continuous communication, or no contact at all. I chose a healthy middle ground of yearly communication - I was definitely excited to meet my biological offspring, but wanted to be wary of getting too attached. Maybe they’ll read this at some point and understand.

On the day of my call with the candidate parents, I found myself stranded in Los Angeles, crashing at my friend’s family home. We had just wrapped up our wild MIT reunion in Las Vegas and flights were delayed by days due to the CrowdStrike July 19th incident. I pulled myself together as much as possible and hopped on a Zoom with Rina and Rishi. All my nerves went away in the first 5 minutes, as I got to hear all about their stories, interests, and goals as parents. I was so delighted for our lives to soon be intertwined.

**The screening (8/4/2024 - 4/24/2025)**

Okay so, the entire screening process took a long time partially due to my hectic schedule and brain half overtaken by founder duties and then starting a new job. The timeline is:

9/4 - My first ultrasound, and a blood test to get everything kicked off. Ultrasound appointments in NYC have to be booked 2 weeks in advance.

10/14 - Genetic screening call #1. I found an email in my spam from Lauren from Connect Genomics, after receiving multiple concerned emails from Erin about my progress.

12/4 - Things with Lauren fell through. Genetic screening call #2, now with GeneScreen. Cofertility and GeneScreen had to investigate how to combine a couple tests to meet specific screening requirements for me.

1/17 - The genetic testing kit was shipped all the way to where I was in Bogotá, Colombia, with my family. I was terrified my grandma would intercept it, and I was on guard for multiple days through shipping delays. Ultimately, Fedex reported delivery was unsuccessful, and it was too late to resolve since I was on my way off to Buenos Aires for a Patagonia trip.

3/4 - Back in NYC, I finally submitted my genetic sample.

3/26 - It was time to do my psychological evaluation. This part was the most fun - I succeeded at convincing the questionnaire that I was mentally sound and not clinically insane. Some of the questions were like: “Do you ever get frustrated that other people get in the way of your success?” and really made me wonder if my alter ego would say yes.

4/14 - My first genetic sample failed. I sent another one, and this one worked, clearing me as an egg donor for Rina and Rishi.

4/24 - I met my future surgery doctor, Dr. Olcha, and he officially gave me medical clearance. The screening work was finally all worth it.

**The legal (4/6 - 5/19)**

This was the part I was least expecting since I hadn’t ever thought about the legal standing of my eggs. I learned donors sign extensive paperwork to give specific rights away to the intended parents. And it’s cool that I received attorney services fully covered.

**The pre-procedure (6/23 - 7/21)**

I was now preparing for the surgery day - 7/22. Over the course of 10 days, I met 15+ different needles for either blood tests or injections and got comfy with ultrasounds. I’ve always been the type to look away during blood tests, and now I had to plunge a needle into my own abdomen for 10 days daily and some days even two times. It went smoothly, except for one injection in which I pierced a blood vessel and saw a horrifying bruise slowly appear over a few days. Despite all, injections are pretty painless, and I got used to the nightly ritual.

Throughout this preparation period, I was on lots of hormones. More than 2x the peak on any given cycle. This led to weirdness, to say the least. My friend reminded me it’s the “pregnancy glow,” so I guess I was partially pregnant during this time. Once I leaned into it, it felt like a superpower - I had never felt so confident and ready to take on the world.

Despite my power-up, I struggled while navigating the terribly fragmented healthcare space. I was communicating with Sama Fertility about my treatment while coordinating pharmacy pickups and appointments with different clinics, and on every interaction I had to explain who my payer was and repeatedly scavenge through my email inbox for CoFertility payment details to use. A couple days before my surgery, I realized I was missing one of my critical medications, and had to rush to coordinate a pharmacy order and pickup in the Upper East Side on a Sunday.

**The procedure (7/22)**

For such a long period of preparation, this surgery went by so fast. I woke up at 5 am to make it to Bergen, NJ, and once I arrived at the clinic I waited for Dr. Olcha to call me in. Soon, I was in the operating room, surrounded by a few nurses, my anesthesiologist, and Dr. Olcha, and immersed in beeping noises and the sound of people asking me to confirm the details of my operation and my name and date of birth. Next, I was breathing into an oxygen tube and felt a needle in my arm, and fell fast asleep.

In my groggy half-woken state, I looked around and assumed the surgery hadn’t happened yet. Time hadn’t passed for me at all. So naturally, I took out my book and started reading to pass the time. After a while, one of the nurses checked in to see if I was ready to start getting dressed. I did a double take, and my brain finally understood that the surgery was over. Everything had gone well.

**Recovery (7/22-7/31)**

My friend Jaclyn came to meet me and we walked out of the clinic into a sparkling sunny day in suburban New Jersey. We chatted all the way home on the ACE until she handed me off to my roommate back at our East Village place. I was ecstatic to be done with meds and clinics, and made myself glorious scrambled eggs (pun not intended) with toast. Later in the day, I received an email with stellar extraction results - meaning 1-2 expected kids for me and 1-2 expected kids for Rina and Rishi.

I was in the trenches during the recovery process. It felt like a combination of IBD, a UTI, and period cramping. I couldn’t drink or eat much before my belly felt way too full, so I was on constant trips to the bathroom, and all I wanted to do was sleep. Good friends and a long Saturday at Central Park cured me.

Now that I’m past egg freezing, it feels like I’ve checked off a huge life to-do. I’ve invested in my future family by preserving my highest quality eggs for later use, whenever I decide to have kids. My parents and grandma might still be warming up to the idea of a small Daniela floating around in the next year or so, but I feel so much joy for Rina and Rishi and can’t wait to get a picture or two at some point.
