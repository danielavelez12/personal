**U.S. financial services and the global order**

My view on the global order is based on a combination of Ray Dalio and Nassim Taleb’s perspectives — it’s driven by a set of cyclical factors and unexpected shocks, but countries can reduce these effects by being antifragile. The question is how prepared the U.S. is to handle shocks like external or internal conflict or market events. A few things make me optimistic:

1. **The U.S. has growing energy stability.** We’re seeing energy stability become increasingly important, and in the next ten years it’ll be crucial to keep our AI workforce, tooling, and systems running.
2. **The U.S. and China have a relatively stable relationship (knock on wood).** The U.S. and China still depend heavily on each other, and even if that interdependence decreases, the threat of mutually assured destruction helps maintain a global order of peace and reduce the chances of significant external conflict.
3. **The U.S. is well positioned to open up economic opportunities for lower socioeconomic classes.** The AI revolution will displace a large portion of the population, the same way the industrial revolution did, but the U.S. has the educational infrastructure to retrain workers and the natural resources to expand its supply across different industries. This will help the U.S. manage internal socio-economic conflict.

It’s unclear how the U.S. will fare relative to other countries, though, especially those with powerful emerging markets. However, U.S.-based financial services companies that have a growing global reach, such as [JP Morgan](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/jpmorgan-commercial-bank-enters-singapore-israel-global-push-2023-06-21/), [MasterCard](https://newsroom.mastercard.com/news/press/2024/october/citi-and-mastercard-join-forces-to-transform-global-cross-border-payments/), and [Stripe](https://www.linqto.com/unicorn-news/stripes-global-expansion-new-partnerships-and-market-penetration/), are well positioned to succeed regardless of the U.S.’s position in the world order.

**Changing consumer expectations**

To understand how consumer expectations will change in the next 10 years, we can look at today’s Gen-Z.

1. **They’re chronically online and digitally native.** Large banks will fall behind if they don’t innovate on their interfaces and UX, and small banks will partner with fintechs and have an edge here. The more agile financial institutions will prevail through the AI revolution.
2. **Regardless, they appreciate in-person experiences.** Gen-Z visits bank branches [more](https://www.cnn.com/2019/11/20/business/bank-branches-millennials-adobe/index.html) often than millennials and boomers. They want to get help with their banking or financial needs and value a friendly, authentic experience.

Financial institutions need to adopt an omni-channel strategy — reaching consumers where they are with both quality digital interfaces and authentic in-person experiences.

Gen-Z consumers also [seek](https://www.pymnts.com/credit-unions/2024/52-percent-of-gen-z-and-millennial-consumers-say-they-might-give-community-banks-a-try/) personalization in their financial services and customer support, moving them to consider smaller financial institutions. They want their bank to know them and understand their community.

**Fintech trends**

In general, fintech is evolving due to growing capabilities and consumer expectations. The fintech market will continue to be lucrative because people are willing to pay for faster funds flows, personalized services, better experiences, etc.
