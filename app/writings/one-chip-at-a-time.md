My one close-up action shot from high school basketball is of me, jumping to catch a rebound with my arms extended, except the ball is hitting me in the face instead of landing in between my hands. I played basketball for about seven years of my life, during elementary school and a couple years in high school, following in my the footsteps of my dad, a former college basketball athlete. Except out of all the things I was involved in as an overachiever only child, basketball was my biggest source of shame. On my 17th birthday, my parents came to our game and took me to dinner afterwards, but I just went to the restaurant bathroom and cried. So many game moments flashing in my head - messy passes, off-the-mark shots, and a lot of scenes in which I had the ball in my hands and simply froze. I loved basketball, but I couldn’t seem to improve. I recently realized, though, it’s never too late to get those chips off of your shoulder.

One of the first things I did in NYC was buy a basketball and find a court to play at. For the first year and a half of NYC, I would occasionally go practice my shooting but was too scared to join games, even when added to group chats to play with friends. This past summer, I decided it was time to push myself back onto he court. So I signed up for a rec league, and was placed on a team with 10 others for a season.

The team was made up of a good mix of folks - co-ed, all types of backgrounds, but all solid players with a competent 3-point shot, powerful layups, and fierce defense. I again felt the looming fear of not being good enough and letting my team down. But I also felt excited, because I finally had another opportunity to prove what I hadn’t been able to as a kid.

The season is coming to a close, and I’ve surmounted a lot of my anxieties and even pulled some weight in our recent games. Though I haven’t fully reached a point I’m proud of yet, I’ve learned a lot about picking things back up - sports or any skills - and actually improving.

**You have to really want it.**

My time spent playing basketball and doing other activities as a kid wasn’t really directed towards goals. I was doing what I was told - doing things for my parents, or school, or to impress my peers. And of course, to get into college. But I didn’t have any goals in mind with basketball, other than spend time doing it. As I’ve been detoxing from the people-pleaser, college prep school kid mindset, I’ve realized innate drive is everything. An internal desire to improve leads to focused practice, which makes all the difference.

Now, I know my goal is to be able to have fun in a game. Being able to hold my own against the average person at the street courts, making 1-2 or more shots per game, and being able to confidently shoot 3-pointers in games. Of course, it’s not a huge goal like somehow making money from basketball or being a rec league team captain, but it’s a reasonable goal I can set my mind to for the next year or so. Long-term, I’ve visualized myself hitting the neighborhood courts on work evenings or weekend mornings throughout the next 10-20 years of my life, including making it one of the family sports. And it’s something I really want, not for anything or anyone else but for myself.

As a result, I’m so much more locked in. I’m curious to watch games whenever possible, even though I’ve never been into watching sports. I head to the courts before work, on early weekend mornings, or on Friday nights, when I would’ve otherwise been enjoying myself with friends, because I feel so excited to watch my technique improve. It’s an all-encompassing mindset. I feel love for my basketball shoes, I catch myself looking at my basketball tenderly, and I feel alive on the basketball court.

**Challenge the status quo, be curious, and find knowledge from within you.**

When trying to improve on a childhood hobby, you might have echoes in your head of advice (or scolding) from coaches. Or maybe you’ve just done something a lot and it feels familiar. All of this subconscious knowledge is actually really useful if you put it into action.

As I’ve been practicing my shooting and wondering why my shots are so wildly inconsistent, I’ve been cycling through the knowledge in the back of my head - rainbow into the net, practice with only the right hand, don’t shoot from the chest, think about a spring motion. I treated my shooting like a lab experiment. When does the ball go farther with less effort? How do I make the force vectors align to give the ball the right vertical lift? If I shoot correctly, what should my end form look like? I tried applying re-interpreting the different pieces of advice and asking myself questions to come up with more hypotheses to test.

One weekend morning, after about 12 hours of cumulative intentional practice, it all started to click. One of the sequence of movements I tried resulted in a perfect shot that felt incredibly effective and effortless, compared to the crooked, effortful shots I was taking before. By positioning my right arm under the ball like a restaurant server’s arm carrying a tray, then combining a spring motion with a jumpshot, then making sure my right hand ends extended in the direction of the ball, I became an almost-mechanical ball launcher.

I’m sure something like private coaching would do miracles for me, but I’ve surprised myself with how much I’ve been able to auto-improve by just paying more attention to things.

**Technique is 80% of the battle.**

This is something I’ve realized through things like climbing and piano as well. In basketball, I had gaslit myself into thinking I wasn’t tall or strong enough to be a good player. Technique takes a lot of time to build and ingrain into muscle memory, but any improvement in technique will significantly boost performance. Without being able to life more than 50 lbs at the gym, I’ve been able to consistently make three pointers with my technique improvements.

Good technique means you’re being as efficient as possible with turning your energy into force, motion, and outcomes. Otherwise you’re wasting energy spending it in the wrong ways, and capping your potential. It’s super specific to what you’re doing in some ways, but in other ways super transferable (e.g. follow-through really matters in most sports). And it’s specific to you - like your “beta” in climbing. Figuring out what technique works for you is a puzzle worth solving.

**Rewrite your story.**

I recently listened to a book about the art of storytelling. It shared a case study in which each participant had a split brain due to an injury, so the right side of the brain operated independently from the left. In one case, the researchers prompted the person to stand up by pulling on their left hand, unnoticed by the right side of their brain, and then asked the person why they were standing up. After the person’s right side of the brain processed the question, they responded they were going to grab a coke from the fridge. The brain casually made up an entire lie instead of thinking the person might’ve been coerced or pulled to stand up.

I think about this a lot now. On one hand, I’ve trusted myself more with my instincts, but on the other, I’ve doubted every story my brain comes up with. When my thoughts are no longer just gut feelings and wander into creative storytelling land, I know to question myself.

A story I’ve told myself is the one about my basketball shortcomings. Telling myself I was just an uncoordinated nerd, or that I was always underestimated and disregarded by my coaches, or that I’m terrible at making decisions under pressure. The truth is, I just didn’t spend enough time and energy on basketball, and I was spread thin trying to do too many things. Unlearning these stories has helped me regain my agency and start to fix my mistakes from before in order to actually reach my goals.
